### ROI or Return on Indecision

Last week, Bain & Company released a report noting disappointing ROI from AI in most enterprise settings. It caught on quickly in our polarized climate, where you’re either an AI accelerationist or an AI doomer. Many used it to argue there’s no real enterprise benefit from AI. I read the full report and found it leaned on a so‑called MIT “study” that was really just conference interviews, along with other unsupported claims popular with AI skeptics. This is odd, given that a previous Bain article is well written and well researched on why AI isn’t seeing the mass adoption in legacy enterprises many expect. It’s worth a read — I’ve linked it here: [https://www.bain.com/insights/your-ai-budget-is-growing-your-returns-arent-heres-why/](https://www.bain.com/insights/your-ai-budget-is-growing-your-returns-arent-heres-why/)

![Figure](/images/blog/2026-06-08-return-on-indecision/image-02.png)

Like any new powerful, flexible technology, people try to force AI into neat, measurable buckets out of misunderstanding or ignorance. But, as with any tool, some applications make sense and others offer little real value. Leaders responsible for AI in the enterprise must know where these tools work well and where they shouldn’t be applied. Complicating this, measuring ROI for AI is genuinely difficult.

Current critiques of ROI measurement center on mismatched metrics, flawed sequencing, and gaps between projections and outcomes.

-   Proxy metrics often overshadow true P&L impact: hours saved, queries processed, users onboarded, adoption rates, and similar “green dashboards” don’t answer the core question — what’s the impact on revenue, cost reduction, margins, or profitability? Individual productivity may rise, but organization-level results often lag.
-   There’s a paradox between productivity gains and context/workflow debt. Surveys from Reuters and Deloitte show about 66% of organizations report productivity and efficiency gains, yet only around 20% see revenue impact. AI systems lack the implicit organizational context humans use — team norms, history, and decision-making processes — which matters most outside clean domains like coding. Individual time savings rarely compound into margin expansion or top-line growth without deliberate context engineering, memory systems, and workflow redesign.
-   From the link above, 951 companies found that while 37% aimed for 11%–20% cost savings, nearly 40% achieved only 0%–10%. Many projections assume fully autonomous agents and AI workflows, yet just 7% of deployments are truly autonomous; most require human approval or ongoing guardrails. Data access and integration remain the top barrier — something I’ve emphasized for months. Ultimately, most companies still approach AI as an IT project instead of the business and process transformation it truly is.
-   Companies also struggle to quantify results. Recent CB Insights Enterprise surveys show 40–57% of organizations can’t track or quantify AI or agent ROI, relying on narratives or “vibes.” Domains with discrete, countable outputs — such as software development, legal contract reviews, and customer service throughput — fare better; most others do not.

There’s a lot of MBA-speak around recommendations, but basically to improve ROI, focus on execution fundamentals. Reduce workflow debt by redesigning processes, emphasizing data, throughput, and clear workflow descriptions before introducing AI. Only about 21% of adopters do this, yet it significantly raises the odds of achieving >5% EBIT impact.

Start with bounded, accessible workflows where AI can help address data issues. Shift the operating model so humans move into orchestration or supervision roles. Measure at the enterprise level (decisions made, customer outcomes, and responses) rather than isolated program savings, and assign clear governance ownership.

The conversation is shifting from “adopt AI” to “prove and scale value.” Credible voices emphasize that ROI issues rarely stem from models or tools. They come from people, processes, data foundations, context, and disciplined measurement. When these are sequenced and measured correctly — when the architecture is right — AI shifts from an expensive experiment to a compounding enterprise advantage.

### What’s your workflow worth?

[https://x.com/MTSlive/status/2060447529133867339?s=20](https://x.com/MTSlive/status/2060447529133867339?s=20)

People often note that different foundational models from different labs perform better or worse on specific tasks. Of course they do. Each lab trains on different data and targets different markets. Still, most frontier labs reportedly spend $10–15 billion on high-quality data.

For example, if you have a particularly complex, valuable workflow in your SAP S4 instance, it could be worth up to $500,000 as customized training data. This is because such data isn’t publicly available in a way models can learn from at real depth.

So, if you use multiple vendors, always track which model provider best fits your enterprise needs or specific workflows.

After Uber publicly admitted it blew through its yearly token budget in three months, I started wondering…what is Uber building that needs so much new code to burn that many tokens? The same question applies to apps like Airbnb or Lyft. Unless you’re building net new features or modernizing an older codebase for efficiency, what exactly are you spending all this money on?

### Attachment

I recently read that top uses for ChatGPT include life advice, counseling, and companionship. That’s not how I use AI, but in today’s increasingly atomized society, I can see why people might lean on it as a stand-in for human interaction. I don’t think that’s especially healthy or something I’d recommend, but intellectually it makes sense.

I’ve also run into an interesting case myself. During a shift from an OpenAI enterprise setup to Microsoft Copilot, the main objection wasn’t the hassle of migrating workflows or customizations. It was that people felt ChatGPT knew and understood them. This introduces an unforeseen psychological and emotional change-management challenge for enterprises. It’s solvable, but it’s notable — and, to me, unexpected.
