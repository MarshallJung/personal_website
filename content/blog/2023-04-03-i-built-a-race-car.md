Hello! And welcome to my…tens of subscribers?

It occurred to me that while everyone was so terribly impressed with my amazing driving videos ([https://www.youtube.com/channel/UCCYYfBZ4nLjst04g8RpKNNA](https://www.youtube.com/channel/UCCYYfBZ4nLjst04g8RpKNNA)), I hadn’t actually told anyone about the car specifically.

There is history to the project and it’s been part of my life for some considerable time. You know how when you are young you often make decisions that, at the time, seem to be pretty logical and straightforward?

Yea, well…that was what the decision to build this car seemed at the time. I had purchased this 944 in a less than ideal condition and after thinking I’d rebuild it I came to grips with the cost of using original Porsche parts and that dream deflated faster than a sad kid’s birthday balloons.

![Figure](/images/blog/2023-04-03-i-built-a-race-car/image-01.jpg)

So as it sat next to my co-workers Corvette I noticed that they shared about the same wheelbase. Thus was hatched a plan to put the running gear from a Corvette under the 944. A whacky idea 15 years ago and still highly questionable today. I truthfully don’t know of any other in existence.

So after getting a totalled Corvette rolling chassis I cut the entire bottom out of the 944 and dropped it over the top. I worked with a buddy of mine to design the roll cage and mandrel bent and welded the cage before permanently affixing the Porsche body in place.

![Figure](/images/blog/2023-04-03-i-built-a-race-car/image-02.jpg)

Let me be upfront here, as we are amongst friends. Every part of this car, save for the transmission, was purchased in its most basic parts and assembled, or completely fabricated from scratch by me. I owe a ton to my brother who helped me with lots of grimbly labor as well as an occasional eye to let me know that I was doing something overly complicated and silly.

So let’s dig in and talk about the various systems and engineering on the car. I’ll attempt to tell you why I did what I did. I don’t claim to be an expert, merely an armchair genius! So go ahead and throw some tomatoes in the comments if you have a better idea…(not bloody likely)

### Aerodynamics

Many of the design considerations came about because of the desire to run aggressive aero. From the beginning I knew that I definitely wanted to have a flat floor with appropriately sized splitter and diffuser. The floor is built from marine plywood (or as we call it in the biz: “natural carbon fiber”). Diffuser strakes and guide fences were made from HDPE plastic that stock car racers use to effect body repairs to damaged cars. It’s really quite tough and cheap. These side skirts rub the ground at speed sealing the higher pressure air on the sides of the car from migrating underneath.

![Figure](/images/blog/2023-04-03-i-built-a-race-car/image-03.jpg)

The large vertical spoiler was chosen as an aid for both rear downforce as well as to help create a high pressure zone on top of the rear heat exchanger. This pressure differential helps move air through the tunnel and exit into the lower pressure zone behind the car.

![Figure](/images/blog/2023-04-03-i-built-a-race-car/image-04.jpg)

So far the car has seemed very stable at speed, especially under throttle. The center of pressure for the underfloor is under the engine, but there may be a need for a wing in the future. Time will tell.

### Suspension

Let me first lead off with the tires as they constitute a very important part of the suspension. I chose to go with 17 inch tires, mostly because they had larger side walls and these side walls could help absorb higher frequency irregularities allowing for the rest of the suspension to be tailored for the lower frequency movements. I have 275 Nitto NT05’s up front and 315 NT05’s in the rear.

The car uses a lightly modified version of the original Corvette suspension. This consists of dual control arms in the front and a SLA with dual trailing arms in the rear all bushed with hard poly. Springing is provided by transverse fiberglass leaf springs front and rear. Dampers are from the pavement stock car world and consist of single adjustable Koni’s front and rear in custom mounting locations via pushrods and rockers in a 2:1 ratio. With low overall wheel travel greater control was achieved by running the dampers over a greater travel range. The front suspension carries the stock swaybar, while the rear has a custom setup.

![Figure](/images/blog/2023-04-03-i-built-a-race-car/image-05.jpg)

![Figure](/images/blog/2023-04-03-i-built-a-race-car/image-06.jpg)

Now I’m going to explain why I built the suspension the way I did. This has some level of suspension theory that I’m sure will raise hackles and start food fights. There is no agreement on this and I don’t think the cats and dogs will ever live together so let’s dive right in.

I built the car with the idea of running zero-droop. This is effected by using limiting straps on the front and rear of the car. This means that the car sitting statically will have no downward wheel travel and should you jack up a corner the wheel will immediately come off the ground.

Every suspension design is built for a particular intended use. On smooth racetracks putting power to the ground does not usually have to contend with undulations where the wheel will be coming off the ground. By limiting the downward wheel travel, in cornering the weight transfer of the car will be forced to try and lift the inside wheels off the ground around the center mass axis of the car. This might be 1000 pounds or more. By limiting travel the inside of the car will help keep the car flatter and more predictable. Note that this does not appreciably reduce grip since the inside tires are not generating much grip at the limit of adhesion. And let’s face it, I never go around any corner at anything less than exactly the limit of adhesion.

Lastly I’ll mention the interaction of the suspension and the aero. The leaf springs are progressive, but probably too soft at higher compression. I run multicellular urethane bump stops front and rear with known compression rates. After looking at some go-pro footage At higher speeds on the straights the spring is mostly compressed with aero loads and any additional compression is taken up by the bump stop. This setup allows soft springing for better mechanical grip in slower speed corners with higher spring rate to keep the car from totally blocking up when aero load is applied.

### Drivetrain

As has likely been obvious through the video so far, much of the build has been constructed using materials and parts that are readily available and inexpensive. I use air quotes there because “inexpensive” is relative when dealing with cars.

In that vein, the drivetrain follows a similar path with available and inexpensive parts. The heart of the matter is a 350 cubic inch (5.7 liter) small block Chevy V8. When I began building this car the Chevy LS wasn’t really a big thing yet (if that tells you how long ago that I began this project!) so this engine was the go-to for reliable inexpensive performance. An aluminum block LS would likely shave another 100 lbs off the weight of the car, but cei la vie…

![Figure](/images/blog/2023-04-03-i-built-a-race-car/image-07.jpg)

The engine has a decent spec hydraulic roller cam and upgraded pistons and rods. It was specced to make a super flat 400 lb/ft of torque from about 2500 rpm through about 5800. This was prioritized to make the car much more drivable should I find myself in the wrong gear. It’s fuel injected with an electric water pump. The cooling is provided via a dual radiator setup. One in the back seen previously as well as this smaller unit up front. On really hot days the rear mounted radiator was simply not able to keep up with hard loading. Design fail on my part. The dual setup probably costs significant additional weight.

Backing the engine is a 18 lb. flywheel and performance clutch attached to a Tremec TKO 500 5-speed transmission. Behind that is an aluminum driveshaft and the 3.73 clutch limited slip rear end.

One last point is that the engine and drivetrain is all solidly mounted. So far this hasn’t been too bad as the engine seems to have good balance, though in some of my race track videos the vibration will come through on the footage, especially at higher RPM’s.

### Interior

OK…last stop. Here is the office. Not too much to say really. The chairs are from Kirkey and mounted to the roll cage. I opted for a lexan rear firewall, not that I can keep track of anything back there while driving, but it does make the cockpit a tad less claustrophobic.

![Figure](/images/blog/2023-04-03-i-built-a-race-car/image-08.jpg)

Pedal setup is from Wilwood, as are the brakes. Dual master with balance bar. Custom throttle pedal.

OK, steering. I retained the Corvette rack and pinion, but have a new steering column with solid needle bearing universal joints. This feeds into a 2:1 steering quickener. This allows the car to go from lock to lock without taking my hands off the steering wheel. It is lovely on the track, but don’t sneeze while driving it on the road or you’ll change two lanes without a second’s notice. Obviously power steering is absolutely necessary as the effort at the wheel through the quickener and the big front tires is…um…nigh on impossible even with these guns (grrrr…)

Anyone following my channel (mostly my friends…and probably my cat) is aware that the power steering has been something of an issue. Hopefully solved, but when it did fail at the track, getting the car back to the pits was…the pits…hmmph.

Electrical systems are all custom built. I was going to build the whole switch panel and breakers myself, then realized that I was being dumb and bought this power distribution module. All MOSFET. Programmable switches, circuit breakers and 30 amp rating per channel with a one wire hookup…yea… Best. Decision. Ever. Not one single electrical issue so far.

Gauges. Simple tach, oil pressure, fuel, water temp are enough to keep track of vitals. The computer displays ECM parameters here on this tablet. I also have a GPS speedometer to make sure I’m not being a total jerk on the public roads.
