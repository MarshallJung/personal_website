It has been just over a week since significant developments unfolded in the AI M&A space, marked by a dramatic and intricate (some might say controversial) deal between Windsurf and Google. In April, OpenAI had been in advanced talks to acquire Windsurf, an AI coding startup, for $3 billion, but the deal collapsed due to tensions with its major investor, Microsoft. Seizing the opportunity, Google invested approximately $2.4 billion to recruit Windsurf’s top talent and leadership, who will now drive AI advancements at DeepMind. Meanwhile, the remaining Windsurf entity, now employee-owned, retains full rights to its cutting-edge technology and continues to operate independently.

This is a pivotal shift in AI-driven M&A: the competition is increasingly about securing top-tier talent rather than just acquiring technology. We can tack the Windsurf drama onto the Meta hiring Google’s move highlights how companies are prioritizing the human ingenuity behind proprietary solutions over the tools themselves. As software development becomes a marginal cost, creativity, product taste, problem-solving skills, and innovative ideas emerge as the true currency. These are, for now, uniquely human skillsets. Other AI startups should take note: in a landscape where tech giants like Google, Amazon, MSFT vie for dominance, talent is the ultimate prize. Is it worth $$BB, though? Probably not in itself, but if the hiring can restructure the internal teams in a way to capitalize on the fast moving development (something that big companies like Google/Meta/etc. are bad at doing) then the ROI might be there. That’s a pretty big bet on culture, but it’s interesting to see that the big players are putting their chips down on this one.

And not to put too fine a point on it, but the near universal adoption of CLI tools like Claude Code for the e/acc crew PLUS seeing a16z burn a bull post on X over Cursor hints to me that this might just be happening.

And on the back of the Windsurf deal falling through, OpenAI announced their “transformative” $10B consulting package: [https://www.forbes.com/sites/solrashidi/2025/07/16/openais-10m-ai-consulting-business-deployment-takes-center-stage/](https://www.forbes.com/sites/solrashidi/2025/07/16/openais-10m-ai-consulting-business-deployment-takes-center-stage/)

OpenAI didn’t just pivot into consulting. They quietly admitted their core product isn’t enough to stand on its own. It indicates that their current business model, like other API-based offerings, is not self-sufficient. This strategy, previously employed by Palantir, suggests that enterprise AI is fundamentally a services business. The future of AI deployment likely relies on extensive human intervention to integrate models into existing, often inefficient, client workflows, effectively replicating traditional “digital transformation” and “strategic partnership” high-margin consulting services.

### TANSTAAFL

![Figure](/images/blog/2025-07-21-the-game/image-03.jpg)

I apologize for this week’s newsletter focusing heavily on gut feelings, but it’s crucial to understand how the community is engaging with current AI tools. This insight, combined with first principles from software dev, psychology and business, can help us intuit what’s happening behind the scenes.

As illustrated in the screenshot, there’s a level of obscurity with some pro-AI coding tools. While users initially praise them for delivering thousands of dollars in value for just $200 a month, they soon start sensing something is off. When the same company controls both the inference supply and the underlying mechanisms for that inference, and users can’t access the code driving that inference, you start to get some perverse incentives. Or at least not aimed at “best coding agent possible.”

Companies may be prioritizing sustainable unit economics over delivering the best coding solutions. We don’t know if their performance has changed. Did they swap models, add latency, reduce context, or swap to a quantized input path? The inherent non-determinism of transformer based algorithms means variable outcomes are to be expected, but this uncertainty raises a bigger question: is the performance truly degraded, or is it just unclear?

### A Different Recipe

@AndrewYNg, cofounder of Coursera and ex-Google Stanford CS professor said recently at a talk for YC Startup School, “I don’t see product management work becoming faster at the same speed as engineering. I’m seeing this ratio shift. Just yesterday, one of my teams came to me, and for the first time, when we’re planning headcount for a project, this team proposed to me not to have 1:4 PM/engineers, but to have 1:0.5 PM/engineers. I still don’t know if this is a good idea, but for the first time in my life, managers are proposing having twice as many PMs as engineers. I think it’s a sign of where the world is going.”

For many years, product managers have been viewed as engineers who just couldn’t make it. However, with the rise of AI’s capabilities in structured tasks like coding, product managers have made a real comeback. With an advantage that they understand the unique intersection of human interaction with technology. This blend of expertise, encompassing human psychology, business, and technology, presents a significant opportunity for these types of creative generalists to effectively leverage AI.

### DSPy

[https://github.com/stanfordnlp/dspy](https://github.com/stanfordnlp/dspy)

I’ve been experimenting with DSPy, a Python library that aims to address the non-deterministic nature of large language models by treating inputs as a programming language instead of just writing natural language. I think it has its place, but often isn’t the answer to building an AI solution. Perhaps it’s best suited to automating small, well defined agentic tasks in a toolchain.

As many of my long-time readers know, I believe good writing correlates with good thinking. Taking the time to craft a quality prompt or provide coherent feedback on a model’s specific failures is crucial. Without it, essential human thinking is lost. You can optimize an evaluation, but that won’t uncover unknowns. It’s important to review data and reflect on what’s happening, as research shows criteria drifts after analyzing LLM traces. [arxiv.org/abs/2404.12272](http://arxiv.org/abs/2404.12272) You shouldn’t simply write requirements and hand them off to an LLM. Injecting your taste and judgment through writing is key, as programming often misses the nuanced vision in your mind.
