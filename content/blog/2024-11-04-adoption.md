The Fed, Harvard and Vanderbilt released a report on the adoption of AI in the US. The abstract, and let’s be honest that’s all most folks will read, makes the claim that 40% of US adults are using generative AI. But that percentage subsumes people who asked any of the major LLMs even the most basic of queries just ONCE in the last month. Only “after the fold” is the fact that only 0.5% — 3.5% of work hours involved generative AI assistance, translating to 0.125–0.875 percentage point increase in labor productivity.

[https://static1.squarespace.com/static/60832ecef615231cedd30911/t/66f0c3fbabdc0a173e1e697e/1727054844024/BBD\_GenAI\_NBER\_Sept2024.pdf](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/60832ecef615231cedd30911/t/66f0c3fbabdc0a173e1e697e/1727054844024/BBD_GenAI_NBER_Sept2024.pdf)

![Figure](/images/blog/2024-11-04-adoption/image-02.jpg)

Given the hype and massive $$$ investment already in the AI space this is a terrible ROI. The paper compares the rate of adoption of the PC and the internet to AI tools and comes away with the conclusion that AI is being adopted at nearly a 2x rate. There are problems with these comparisons, which need some nuance, but I think it might be fair to say that the standardized labor productivity boost was likely higher with previous technologies. Now, you might say. “…Marshall, are you not a proponent of AI? Why the negativity?” I AM a proponent, and I do think this is a revolutionary technology. However, the hype cycle needs to reset. It would be bearish for AI with a 40% adoption rate across all potential customers, given the current over investment, unprofitability and low total revenue of the industry. The potential upside comes from the fact that GenAI does \*not\* have very high adoption right now, and that it is growing. I don’t think we have found that “magic” publicly available and understandable AI application yet. NotebookLM I think was the real first application. But there will be more. Stick around, pay attention to the hypsters, and the dark horses. A bit of flush on the VC side and we’ll come out the other end.

### Unverified productivity

So here’s a white pill to follow up the red one from the previous section. The founder of [https://www.lindy.ai/](https://www.lindy.ai/) ([https://x.com/Altimor](https://x.com/Altimor)) says he was shown an internal AI tool for a company that, “…built an AI agent that: 1. makes phone calls to everyone in the co every week, asking for a status update. 2. Sends a report back with a summary of all the updates. 3. Has a convo so the CEO can double click on any issue. Basically replaces the entire middle management layer of the business — no human needed to distill information up and down, with all the biases & politics & slowness involved in that. Just ICs, a CEO, and AI in the middle.”

Now, readers of the newsletter know that I use an AI productivity stack that includes audio recording, transcription, e-mail/calendar exports, LLM keyword identification, Obsidian ingestion, weekly summaries and now NotebookLM podcast week-in-review. If this can be packaged and sold as, at a minimum, a team productivity tool I think we can see those low productivity hours from the FED/HArvard report bump up significantly.

### It’s Still an Expert’s Field

According to the following paper: [https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=66018](https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=66018) the younger oncoming generation of graduates/workers actually can’t do all that well with generative AI. Why? It’s the sad fact that they seem to lack precise, well written and understandable English language skills. When trying to describe a task where the embedded information is distilled from curated domain expertise, 733t 5p3ak and text pidgin simply won’t get you the results. Speaking as someone that loves writing and is constantly frustrated by my lexical inadequacies I understand the issue all too well. I’ve noticed that today’s top AI models work best when guided by domain specialists. For example, someone without expertise in fields like mine (aerodynamics, materials science, programming or mechanics) might miss small nuances that make an AI’s response subtly incorrect. Non-experts often struggle to ask precise questions that elicit truly professional answers from AI. That’s why, at this stage, I see current AI models as ideal “coworkers” for experts rather than standalone solutions.

### Solving stale vectors

Why do vector databases fail? Vector databases treat embeddings as independent data, divorced from the source data from which embeddings are created, rather than what they truly are: derived data. This pitfall means that many AI projects that start out as simple vector search implementations inevitably evolve into a complex orchestra of monitoring, synchronization, and firefighting. Pgai Vectorizer treats embeddings like database indexes. It automatically creates, updates, and maintains embeddings as your data changes. Just like an index, the database handles all the complexity: syncing, versioning, and cleanup happen automatically. Postgres with pgvector has become probably the most popular vector db in production today, especially for folks rolling their own RAG based solutions. This layers with pg vector to solve the update problem. Git here: [https://github.com/timescale/pgai](https://github.com/timescale/pgai)

### Agent for your platform API

[https://github.com/Integuru-AI/Integuru](https://github.com/Integuru-AI/Integuru)

Products need low-latency integrations with other services, but many lack official APIs. Logistics software, financial services, EHRs, government websites, etc. To achieve low latency, developers must reverse-engineer internal APIs. But platforms have different internal API designs, so navigating through each can get complicated. This is an early days example, but ultimately this would speed the development of integration with legacy systems. Didn’t build that API well enough when you were scaling? This might ease the pain.
