The most popular article from my weekly newsletters last year was a standalone piece on NVIDIA’s 2025 GTC Tech Conference in San Jose ([https://medium.com/@jung.marshall/nvidia-gtc-the-dead-walking-a-review-27e5795d487a](https://medium.com/@jung.marshall/nvidia-gtc-the-dead-walking-a-review-27e5795d487a))In that article I argued that many attending startups were already dead because technology was advancing so quickly in directions that favored major players. Many startups were effectively wrappers around API calls to frontier models from big labs, or they offered simple features that fit neatly into the big labs’ tool suites and would soon be subsumed.

In some ways, this also anticipated the “SaaS apocalypse,” which recently saw companies like Workday, Salesforce, and other enterprise SaaS tools get hammered in the public markets.

How closely did last year’s article predictions match this year’s attendance? Scanning the attendee list and specializations, there’s a clear drop in API wrapper companies and a notable rise in industry-specific firms offering niche products that rely on nonpublic or highly specialized datasets, along with a strong focus on hardware, unique chipsets, and robotics. This makes sense because these products or services are either too small or too hard for big companies to pursue given the ROI. It also aligns with the defensibility of AI tied to physical-world manufacturing versus pure software. As the cost of code approaches a marginal cost of zero, software alone might be too commoditized to justify a startup. Especially if it merely adds AI enhancements to an existing workflow.

The shift in companies attending NVIDIA’s GTC is expected. The path forward feels more realistic and sustainable. Posts about the event suggest the AI business and product outlook is more sanguine than the manic enthusiasm of 2025. I’ll keep an eye on a few of my favorite companies from the conference and see how things shape up for 2027.

### Observations from the Trenches

I’ve chatted with numerous enterprises trying to modernize with AI, and while “maturity” is relative, a few patterns are emerging:

-   Agents are the new black, even if nobody agrees on what one is. The shift from mere chatbots to agents is underway. Coding is the initial breakout star (but PM/supply chain, HR are close), suggesting that if an AI can do your least favorite job, that’s where the budget goes first.
-   The “text box” days are over; welcome to “agentic workflow”. Early adopters from verticals across the business are building more. Focusing on better customer outcomes, auto-sourcing insights, and accelerating high-ROI workflows. Cost cutting? That’s a side effect, not the goal. It’s about speeding things up, which is apparently more fun to budget for.
-   Data governance is the true bottleneck. Years of meticulously siloed, fragmented, and gated data, which seemed *so* secure at the time,now actively resist agent adoption
-   AI workflow identity is a nightmare. The “lethal trifecta” (access to data, ability to write/run code, and external communication) is both vitaly important yet difficult to mitigate. Partitioning agent access levels is now required.
-   Token usage costs? Meh, not yet. Budgets aren’t infinite, but for now, the cost of running the actual AI is apparently less concerning.
-   Interoperability is a given. Expect multiple agent systems. Customers are getting wise to this, but convincing internal teams that API-first or MCP interconnectivity requires *different* security reviews than traditional software is a process that involves a surprising amount of paperwork.

### Uncomfortable Truth

I’ve increasingly had to face an uncomfortable and hypocritical truth: I get irritated when others clearly use AI to summarize emails, make points, draft strategy docs, and share information. What used to be the occasional terse, sometimes inscrutable Slack ping has become five or six paragraphs of corpo-speak. Repeatable, identifiable AI prose, heavy on the em dashes. BUT the truth is, I often use AI similarly. I pride myself on not just copy-pasting whatever comes out of the latest version of Claude or GPT, but the added detail from a well-crafted, AI-assisted communication is worth it. That said, I’ve increasingly been told “TL;DR” when I try to communicate important topics. This feels like a small but real case of what Gurwinder Bhogle calls “reality apathy,” where the cost of finding the truth becomes higher than the perceived value of knowing it. At that point, the pliability of people’s beliefs becomes the target of online, AI-generated sloppaganda. To what extent am I contributing to this at work?

### Not Sure

[https://corticallabs.com/](https://corticallabs.com/)

Cortical Labs, an Australian biotech startup, ran Doom on a computer powered by cloned human neurons. Now they’re building two data centers using the same tech: 120 units in Melbourne and, eventually, 1,000 in Singapore. Each “living computer” pairs about 200,000 lab-grown human neurons with a silicon chip and has onboard life support to keep the cells alive for up to six months. They report it uses less energy than a handheld calculator.

I’m honestly not sure what to make of this. A computer made of living human brain cells that can learn and self-organize is being pitched as data center infrastructure. The neurons grow across the chip, receive electrical stimuli, and their responses feed into what Cortical Labs calls a “biological intelligence operating system”, with life support keeping the cells alive.

I don’t know what the ethical framework is for data centers running on human neurons at scale. The cells have to come from somewhere, they’re alive in a meaningful sense, and we’re using them to process information because it’s cheaper than electricity. Maybe that’s fine; maybe it isn’t. I genuinely don’t know, and I’m not sure anyone’s fully thought it through yet.

### Context is King

[https://github.com/andrewyng/context-hub](https://github.com/andrewyng/context-hub)

This open-source tool gives your coding agent up-to-date API documentation. Install it, then prompt your agent to fetch curated docs via a simple CLI. Coding agents often use outdated APIs and hallucinate parameters; this helps prevent that. It also improves over time as agents annotate docs with notes — if an agent finds a workaround, it can save it so it doesn’t have to rediscover it next session.

Beyond a coding app, it’s a living, agent-accessible knowledge base that grows smarter over time through community contributions and per-agent annotations. It also suits project management and documentation. That’s the value of AI tools: with a bit of tangential thinking, they apply far beyond their original intent.

### Agents in Enterprise

[https://x.com/krishnanrohit/status/2032181522296684679](https://x.com/krishnanrohit/status/2032181522296684679)

The author explored how today’s AI agents perform inside real organizations using the open-source Enron dataset.

-   Most Enron employees managed 50–100 concurrent threads with limited context and high organizational knowledge demands.
-   Assigning thread IDs significantly outperformed scratchpad memory alone.
-   Thread IDs plus shared coordination state beat adding more agents or using larger models, with benefits for cost and shared state encoding.
-   This approach is essential to prevent drift, maintain ownership, and preserve reply authorship.
-   Model intelligence still matters; better structure amplifies strong models but can’t rescue weaker ones.

### Careful of the Hype

[https://substack.com/home/post/p-191342187](https://substack.com/home/post/p-191342187)

“Delve built a machine designed to make clients complicit without their knowledge, to manufacture plausible deniability while producing exactly the opposite.”

Delve, a buzzy, AI-first, YC-backed compliance startup, raised $32 million and has been accused of systematically faking SOC 2, ISO 27001, HIPAA, and GDPR compliance reports for hundreds of clients. The above linked Substack reports that a leaked Google spreadsheet with hundreds of confidential draft audit reports shows Delve generating auditor conclusions before any actual review, using the same template for 99.8% of reports, and relying on Indian certification mills operating through empty U.S. shells instead of the U.S. based CPA firms they advertise. Some affected companies are quite large, and a few are ironic. For example, Cluely, known for helping programmers cheat on remote coding tests, was apparently cheated themselves by Delve. The takeaway: there will always be companies willing to cut corners, and it’s easier than ever to do so with “AI” in the sales docs. Enthusiastic skepticism is more essential than ever when evaluating new software companies, new features from existing vendors, or consultant claims.
