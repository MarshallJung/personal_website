Sometimes when I’m talking to a C-Suite customer about Generative AI we are on video, and at other times I’m talking over the phone. But in both cases, where I’m face to face and in the other situations where I’m not, I imagine Dr. David Bowman and the Monolith. What is this black box? What does it do exactly and how does it do it? Can I be reasonably assured that pushing this technology will actually make a difference? As is the case with any enterprise technology decision maker, risk is usually a primary concern. Does the AI tech open the company up to potential legal issues? Can it harm productivity or decision making and how do I know that it has done so? Is this technology going to end up being an albatross around the neck of the company?

Much of this uncertainty stems from the sometimes impenetrable language found in machine learning research and tech articles, and much of it also comes from the fact that there are many “AI Enhanced Technologies” that claim to use AI techniques, but despite the proliferation of different models, products and articles there is precious little research that compares the models and shows why differences might exist. There are good reasons for why this happens and in this article I’ll try and distill some jargon and frame some basic questions that a CTO might want to ask any potential technology provider that comes through the door with the best, newest “AI Enhanced Technology”

![Figure](/images/blog/2023-03-19-help-im-a-cto-and-i-need-generative-ai/image-02.jpg)

To help decode the mystery let’s dive into the first term that will likely be thrown about…”parameters”. It’s taken for granted that one company or another with a model of XXX billion parameters is necessarily better than one with fewer parameters. But is this actually the case. What is a model parameter?

Model parameters reference the internal mathematical structure of a transformer neural network. Each parameter is a number (also referred to as a weight) that is changed during training to either boost or dampen the signal as it passes through the network. A sort of OK analogy would be a stoplight. If you want more cars from one direction to pass you leave the light green for longer in that direction. That would be the equivalent of tuning that stoplight (parameter) to accomplish the task of management. The conventional wisdom is that more parameters means a better AI model. But that isn’t always the case. It depends very greatly on what the task that the model is supposed to perform.

Much of the news cycle has been dominated by the eerily human like performance of conversational AI tools like ChatGPT. While people are indeed trying to build niche tools based off the outputs of ChatGPT, the tool itself was trained and released as a demonstration of general intelligence. The data set ChatGPT was trained on is enormous, somewhere in the petabytes in size. And the dataset consisted of nearly every human topic of knowledge. The complexity of the model is necessary to understand nuance between every different human area of knowledge. This is why ChatGPT has 175 billion parameters.

But what if you don’t need to understand the nuances between the philosophy of John Locke and Immanuel Kant? Perhaps you need to understand only a programming language or a set of langues. Maybe you only need to understand your enterprise codebase? Do you still need all those parameters? Probably not. Does this mean you can get away with a bargain basement model with far fewer parameters if you fine tune the model on your own data…also probably not.

More parameters means more ability to detect nuance and gradation, which does lead to the question of how much is enough. And this is where testing comes in. All ML models should be tested. They should be tested when they are initially trained and especially retrained on a reliable basis when the statistical profile of the model inputs begins to change. So what about testing?

Traditional measures of language model performance like cross entropy, perplexity and benchmarks like BLEU and GLUE tend to be outperformed by the newest LLMs and new model capabilities like few-shot learning and multi-step reasoning are not really accounted for in these methods. Some newer benchmarks like CoQA, LAMBDA, HELLASWAG, LogiQA provide methods for evaluating LLMs for mismatches between the behavior we want LLMs to exhibit and the behavior we observe in practice as a result of the training objectives and data we use. The tools needed to assess LLMs must provide a rigorous, robust, and reproducible framework for practitioners to replicate and investigate published results. The framework must also provide a mechanism for normalizing and decontaminating the training data by removing biased, extraneous, and unwanted elements. Finally, the framework should be capable of detecting and reporting different evaluation versions and preventing data leakage into validation and test datasets.

Does this sound like a lot of work? It IS a lot of work and there are many variables that can change the performance of even one model evaluated against itself. Most enterprise decision makers won’t be able to have much control over some of those variables. As discussed earlier model parameters are pretty well set if you are not training your own LLM from scratch, but there is one arena that can make a big difference. A difference that can even elevate a less complex model above a more complex one and it’s an arena that a business can control. This is the data used to fine tune the base model. If you are interested in using an AI tool for your employees in a particular area of specialization, the data selected does make an enormous difference. Don’t use an LLM fine tuned on medical data to help boost the productivity of your python coders, for example.

While that’s a silly example, it can be more nuanced. My company, Tabnine uses fully-permissive open source code repositories to fine tune our foundation model to be useful for programmers. Deploying Tabnine on-prem at a company and further fine tuning the model on the companies codebase would very likely improve the performance of the tool for the companies programmers. There are other AI tools meant for coding and they are built on different base models and have been fine tuned with different code. Performance characteristics will be different and careful evaluation of fit is necessary.

I write all this to say that in the world of Generative AI powered tools it’s more complicated than simply choosing the biggest model and knowing that it’s going to work the best. The world of testing is lagging the model space. Hampered by the cost of testing, the misalignments of fine-tuning and the sisyphean task of trying to align common data sets across the various models to get a consistent picture of performance, a C-Suite decision maker needs to be a bit more “fine-tuned” in evaluating different AI products.
