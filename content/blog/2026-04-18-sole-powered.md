April 20, 2026

Almost two years ago, I wrote in my newsletter that the AI hype train seemed near its peak after the photo of Jensen Huang signing a woman’s chest at Taiwan’s Computex expo was published. But, as with many of my predictions, I’m often early. So don’t treat my calls as investment advice. Peaks in any cycle can have their own bumps, and occasionally you’re reminded that everyone wants to grab cash while they still can. A recent reality check for the AI gold rush came last week: Allbirds, once a Silicon Valley darling (I’ve always thought the shoes were ugly but then I prefer cowboy boots) announced a dramatic pivot. The company is selling its footwear IP and rebranding as NewBird AI to chase GPU-as-a-service and “neo-cloud” infrastructure, backed by a $50 million convertible financing round.

[https://www.businessinsider.com/allbirds-stock-price-newbird-ai-infrastructure-wall-street-gpus-compute-2026-4](https://www.businessinsider.com/allbirds-stock-price-newbird-ai-infrastructure-wall-street-gpus-compute-2026-4)

Sure, $50 million is a rounding error when competitors are deploying billions, but it shows how thin the line is between an opportunistic pivot and overreach.

Additionally, Goldman Sachs analysts note rising operational friction. As AI scales, enterprises face significant token and inference costs. Some big-tech deployments reportedly exceed entire payrolls. Vendors are rethinking pricing, shifting from per-seat licenses to “units of labor/productivity” to capture value and protect margins. Pilots are cheap; production-scale inference is not.

For a glimpse of how enterprise consumption may change, in the recent Dwarkesh interview, Jensen Huang hinted major frontier labs are considering scaled token pricing based on user-required complexity and speed. Combined with moving away from flat per-seat licensing, this underscores compute constraints and the need to carefully evaluate production-level costs for enterprise AI applications and workflows to ensure they materialize fast enough to justify the financial pivot or budget reallocation.

But before we move on, let’s break this down for the average company adopting AI. It’s unlikely the average employee will get significantly more productive using increasingly expensive AI tools before market economics price them out for broad use. As I’ve said before, effective enterprise adoption means deploying highly focused, narrowly tailored applications within small, enthusiastic, knowledgeable groups that control their own data. That’s where measurable ROI is most viable while AI remains affordable. It will be telling to see when AI tool costs and availability meet the break-even point of an average employee’s productivity gains.

### Palette Cleanser

We’ve outlined the downside in the first section. Now let’s consider the upside. Long-term, the math still favors progress. Rapid model evolution and hardware/software co-design are driving inference costs down sharply. Gartner projects that running a trillion-parameter LLM could be over 90% cheaper in four years, and some models are already 100 times more efficient than their 2022 counterparts. Google’s new open-source Gemma models, for instance, deliver strong performance on a smartphone. Small language models, fine-tuned for specific tasks and running on on-premise hardware, will further improve affordability.

![Figure](/images/blog/2026-04-18-sole-powered/image-02.png)

Open-source momentum, especially from China, is another tailwind. Labs like DeepSeek and Moonshot have released models that rival top Western systems at a fraction of the cost and often run on existing or export-compliant hardware. China’s all-in open-weight strategy is creating a self-reinforcing ecosystem of cheap, customizable AI that lowers application barriers and could keep overall industry costs in check.

Basically, some sensational headlines aren’t doomsday signals. They’re reminders that real economics still apply and are being stress-tested. The future likely belongs to organizations that know when to use costly foundational models and when to invest in the people and infrastructure to run certain models in their own cloud or on-prem hardware.

### Surviving as a SaaS

[https://venturebeat.com/ai/salesforce-launches-headless-360-to-turn-its-entire-platform-into-infrastructure-for-ai-agents](https://venturebeat.com/ai/salesforce-launches-headless-360-to-turn-its-entire-platform-into-infrastructure-for-ai-agents)

![Figure](/images/blog/2026-04-18-sole-powered/image-03.png)

In recent months, there’s been a lot of talk about the death of software-as-a-service. As usual in AI, it’s framed as all or nothing, and many companies have taken big hits to their stock valuations. I’m not saying they’ll all survive in an AI-enabled personal software development future, but the ones that make their systems of record easily consumable by that future will.

Salesforce stands out here. At Valvoline Global Operations, we use Salesforce as our CRM system of record, and I’ve been impressed by their open approach to integrating with other AI tools. They offer a solid set of APIs, MCPs, and AI agents so teams can build what works best for them. This latest move to push even further in that direction is smart — and puts them ahead of the pack.
