### Props, but they’re still scum

The Fake $2.8M Chevron CAIO Brief (And What It Accidentally Got Right About Industrial AI)

There are rarely boring weeks in the AI world, but getting headhunted for a $2.8M Chief Artificial Intelligence Officer seat at Chevron by an LLM enhanced scam definitely puts a new spin on the hype cycle for me personally.

A few days ago, an “executive recruiter” reached out with a five-page, maroon-bannered PDF titled STRICTLY CONFIDENTIAL EXECUTIVE SEARCH: Chief Artificial Intelligence Officer (CAIO) — Chevron Corporation.

On paper, it looked like the exact intersection I’ve spent the last twenty years building toward. Before I spent four years working on machine learning at Google (and before I took the helm as Global Head of AI at Valvoline Global Operations) I spent eleven years in hard hats and steel toes as a field engineer, petrophysicist and senior data scientist at Whiting Petroleum and Halliburton. I went to the Colorado School of Mines. My entire philosophy, Code & Steel, is built around bridging frontier SV software with the physical realities of heavy industry.

So when a brief lands on your desk asking for someone to unify subsurface reservoir characterization, downstream chemical supply chains, enterprise GenAI platforms, and board-level capital allocation, you at least read past page one.

And then my oilfield radar started pinging.

### Anatomy of an AI-Era C-Suite Con

If you’re a VP, CISO, CTO, or technical executive, you’ve probably seen versions of this floating around LinkedIn. It’s a sophisticated evolution of the advance-fee scam, upgraded with frontier LLMs.

Looking closely at the spec, the red flags weren't exactly subtle:

1.  **The Prompt Leakage:** The very first sentence read: "Chevron Corporation is presented here as the target organization..." Ah, yes. Classic Spencer Stuart prose. Or the verbatim output of someone prompting Claude for a fake brief.
2.  **The "Remote C-Suite" Fantasy:** A C-suite role reporting directly to the CEO... remotely from a Colorado ski town. Right. Because if there's one thing energy supermajors love after moving headquarters to Houston, it's remote executives.

Anyway, I sent a resume out of morbid curiosity. Without a single screening call, "Arpita" fast-tracked me to the next stage, provided I pay an offshore "executive-document specialist" to write my dossier.

And there’s the con.

In the real world, retained executive search firms are paid hundreds of thousands of dollars by the client to interview candidates and write the confidential board assessment themselves. They never ask a candidate to pay $2,500 to a third-party "dossier specialist." (As an aside, I’ve always joked that making a great PowerPoint deck would be the true measure of AGI; apparently, generating fake Korn Ferry specs is a close second.)

Amusingly, once you look past the bogus recruiter, the five-page mandate synthesized by their chatbot was remarkably coherent. Mostly because it was trained on the collective panic currently echoing through heavy industry boardrooms and LLM tailored specifically to my public expereince.

CEOs are currently staring into the abyss, wondering why two years of paying for glorified PDF-summarising chatbots haven't trimmed a single day off drilling cycles or stopped a compressor from breaking down.

Should an industrial giant actually appoint a CAIO, here is the gauntlet they would genuinely need to run:

1.  **Build Proper Foundations First:** The spec demanded AI agents for subsurface telemetry and supply chains. Splendid. Except you cannot bolt an autonomous agent onto thirty-year-old ERP schemas and messy sensor data without automating complete chaos at LLM rates. Clean up the data layer before unleashing the digital workforce.
2.  **LLMs guess, don’t calculate:** A 3% hallucination rate in free-sumer software might be acceptable but on a drilling rig, it's potentially a costly disaster. Treat frontier models as probabilistic advisors to narrow search spaces, but lock them inside deterministic physical constraints before letting them near actual machinery.
3.  **Unit Economics:** Making intelligence cheaper causes usage to surge across the business. Don’t route every trivial log lookup through a massive frontier model.
4.  **People Are Harder to Update Than Software:** The real bottleneck isn't silicon. It's persuading the decades long veteran engineer. If you don't spend as much effort training operators as you do fine-tuning models, your expensive platform will probably sit idle.

I shall be politely declining the $3,000 dossier offer. Nevertheless, I'm almost grateful to the scammer's LLM for the exercise. It was a helpful reminder that bridging frontier software with heavy industry requires actual engineering, not just a rather polished PDF. OR I can just send you the job req!

### AI for Science

This library would have been super useful back during my petrophysics days for well log data. It is certainly an interesting way to extract periodic regression predictions from subsurface data extended across different wells in the same basin. Ideally, mismatches would indicate unconformities, additional facies that get added etc., Or support overall predictive maintenance work on the engineering side. Pairing this kind of deterministic approach with AI could really help with existing data analysis and forward prediction. It is definitely another great deterministic tool for the toolbox.
