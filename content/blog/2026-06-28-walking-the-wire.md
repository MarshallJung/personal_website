### Walking the Wire

The drama in AI and the race for the industry’s future are always fascinating. This week, OpenAI released a couple of “smaller” models that landed just shy of Anthropic’s Mythos level still stuck in the purgatory of government ire. OAI conveniently capturing market share while Anthropic navigates their poorly run public relations campaign with the government.

[https://www.theinformation.com/articles/trump-administration-asks-openai-stagger-release-new-model-security-concerns](https://www.theinformation.com/articles/trump-administration-asks-openai-stagger-release-new-model-security-concerns)

As the link above shows, 5.6 was consistently described as being on par with Anthropic’s foremost models. As a result, the government requested a review before release. Credit to Sam Altman, he keeps finding ways to pick up money when it’s on the table.

![Figure](/images/blog/2026-06-28-walking-the-wire/image-02.png)

It’s not all rosy though. The New York Times reports OpenAI is “leaning toward” delaying its IPO until 2027. Recent market volatility has prompted the company to reconsider its timeline, worrying that retail investor enthusiasm may be weak due to sector fatigue, limited available capital, and the SpaceX IPO siphoning attention.

Advisors say OpenAI should either wait until 2027 to aim for a $1 trillion valuation or accept a lower valuation for a faster IPO. The core issue: the company’s valuation relies on its own unaudited internal sales figures. Opting for a lower valuation could signal that sales may not cover current and future capex and other obligations.

### Ohhhh…darn

![Figure](/images/blog/2026-06-28-walking-the-wire/image-03.png)

With AI already seen as pricey, overcharging customers is a bad look. An AI auditing startup reports some customers may be overpaying for Anthropic and OpenAI due to billing inaccuracies. From March to June, 60 companies spending $34 million — mostly on Anthropic’s cloud code — found about $1.7 million in mistaken overcharges.

Common issues were getting billed for newer, pricier models despite using older, cheaper ones. Charged when agents/chatbots didn’t complete requests or returned server-side errors. Unnoticed agent failures that kept retrying tasks (often due to customer, tbf) built agentic loops. Still, it’d be helpful if AI vendors offered safeguards to prevent these loops from hitting customers’ wallets.

### Open Source is Coming

Why should closed-model AI companies worry about accidentally overcharging customers? Because open-source models are improving fast. The Chinese company z.ai released GLM 5.2, and it’s not far behind some frontier models from OpenAI and Anthropic. How they achieve this performance is usually a bit shady. There are reports of large groups of “users” (read mobile phone farms) in China extracting chain-of-thought and reasoning tokens from API interactions to use as reinforcement learning for Chinese open-source models.

If companies can handle 80% of their workload with models that are 30%-50% or more cheaper, they’ll move in that direction. Especially if those models are fully open source and can run on a company’s own infrastructure, shifting spend from variable cost to operating expense. This will put serious pressure on private AI companies.

### Secret Squirrel

[https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract\_id=5464215](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=5464215)

This paper illustrates a point I’ve made in the newsletter: tracking enterprise-wide, individual AI adoption is hard. AI likely boosts overall productivity, but measuring it is difficult. It’s also striking how often people underreport their AI use. The paper calls this “social desirability bias”. If tools, rather than personal intelligence, are seen to drive results, people fear losing respect for their work ethic or ability, so they downplay usage.

Underreporting is common, and many employees bypass AI security and compliance to use personal accounts because they know the tool’s power. To address this, companies should explicitly recognize and reward appropriate, verifiable, and efficient AI use. Treat it as an unbiased good, not a sign of laziness or lower intelligence.

### But, but but

If an organization invests in using AI as a force multiplier and rewards appropriate usage, it won’t be all puppy dogs and roses. Some segments of the workforce will struggle, especially as AI really engages/enables in day-to-day activities in the company. This is largely a human and psychological issue tied to identity (a “carbon bottleneck” issue, not a silicon one). When people define themselves by intelligence or academic achievement, it can be hard to accept that the same level of intelligence may matter less in the future. Being “AGI-pilled” can be very unpleasant, and many intelligent people can’t stomach it. Be cautious of technical leaders who block general AI adoption because they view it as a threat to their status.

### Death of the Apprenticeship

[https://www.pwc.com/gx/en/news-room/press-releases/2026/pwc-2026-ai-jobs-barometer.html](https://www.pwc.com/gx/en/news-room/press-releases/2026/pwc-2026-ai-jobs-barometer.html)

I often give big consulting agencies short shrift on their value add. Still, because they work with many customers, their trend distillation can be useful. The article above outlines how AI is reshaping the global labor market into two paths.

First, AI acts as a force multiplier for experts. Automating routine tasks and amplifying the need for complex human judgment. They’re seeing 2x job growth in roles and 42% faster salary growth. AI has also lowered barriers to entry, making it easier for non-experts to perform previously specialized tasks.

As a result, junior roles now demand senior-level human skills like leadership, creativity, and complex judgment, eroding traditional apprenticeship-style roles.

Finally, the top 20% of companies adopting AI report 163% productivity growth while increasing headcount faster than less-exposed peers (52% vs. 36%).

What does this mean for the average enterprise? First, we need to rethink the talent pipeline. The traditional apprenticeship model is breaking because junior employees no longer learn by doing grunt work. Partner with HR and vertical managers to design training that builds domain expertise, judgment, and critical thinking from day one. Otherwise, you risk a workforce that can use AI but cannot validate underlying physical or business realities.

In parallel, adopt a bimodal training policy: encourage employees to experiment with AI while developing the skills and judgment needed to use agentic workflows properly. This is key to maintaining the entry-level-to-senior leadership pipeline and continuity in the human workforce.
