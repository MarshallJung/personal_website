This Monday’s newsletter will be a bit shorter on the news side, and more heavy on the opinion side. While there were interesting concepts I found while researching, the real news came from the ruckus surrounding Google’s Gemini. If you are as “online” as myself you would have found the ruckus to be expected, at least from a technical perspective, but surprising in as much as the backlash aimed at Google.

But the core issue here is trust. Trust in AI should always be held lightly, after all these products are created by human organizations and we all understand that humans are flawed and broken. Perhaps the concept will be better illustrated by a story. When I worked at Google one of the earliest projects that I worked on was called “Project Maven”. It was work with the DoD to help develop a real time image segmentation and tracking algorithm for UAV’s. The project was good. Good for our troops and good for the communities in which it was meant to operate. I had a chance to work directly with Google core engineers. These folks were incredibly smart, focused and very, very uninterested in anything to do with the politics of the project though it was working with the military. It was simply a complex, unique problem that no one had been able to solve for and they were going to be the first. In the end the project was canceled because people at Google, not involved in the project, were misinformed about its goals and made enough noise to get the plug pulled.

I imagine the same problem solving environment surrounded the team at Google that created Gemini. And they created a wonderful tool. If the initial reports are accurate (and I can attest to a few myself) then Gemini 1.5 is really a step forward equal to the release of GPT4. But things didn’t go very well for Google this week. While Gemini 1.5 wasn’t directly under attack (the Twitter kerfluffle ne internet dragging) came from Gemini 1.0, it does encapsulate the issue of trust. The core question is who pushed Gemini, via sys prompt or training data manipulation into its terribly broken generation? I am quite certain that it was not the core engineering team but middle management. This is where the trust was destroyed. From the project managers to the DEI safety folks, people with minimal technical skills broke Gemini.

I find myself trusting individuals more than organizations. For an individual there are enough external and social cues to establish trust. That isn’t to say that I would trust everything any one person says, but it is easier to tell. In an organization individuals with antisocial behavior traits are incentivized to “play the game”, advancing their careers through the minefield of red tape and politics. This leads to untrustworthy people leading tech companies like Google. Concentrated in middle management, they are more concerned with social impact and politics than the end user. I am quite certain that this phenomenon is what happened this week and it will not be solved until there is a trustworthy leader that deliberately and publicly changes the company’s course.

As one Twitter user put it succinctly, “We created a technology that has the power to answer nearly any question that humanity might imagine in a nearly person like manner and the first thing we asked it to do was to lie to us.”

### Related to trust

[https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228797158\_How\_complex\_systems\_fail](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228797158_How_complex_systems_fail)

When systems become so complex that understanding them is largely outside the realm of expertise of any one individual, the potential for failure is significantly higher. Backup processes and systems, multiple layers of failsafes are always needed, but too often humans are part of this loop as well. When we rely on people the potential is escalated even more. Since what are humans than complex and unpredictable systems ourselves?

### Tokenization basics

[https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zduSFxRajkE](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zduSFxRajkE)

In this (not) short tutorial, Karpathy goes through the GPT tokenizer build from scratch. I think this is an underappreciated aspect of LLMs and is something that I have been talking about for some time especially in my Medium article ([https://medium.com/@jung.marshall/the-language-of-embeddings-8976e3779446](https://medium.com/@jung.marshall/the-language-of-embeddings-8976e3779446)). How we “break up” any external concept into a language that can then be learned by a NN is something that I think is where the next meaningful step in LLMs will come from. Take for example the 3D universe. What is the language of a racecar? Can the cartesians and material properties of each of the component parts be atomized in a way that makes the language better suited to an attention transformer? I think so. It is a project that I think would make a good weekend effort at some point.
