AI is reshaping the assumptions about modern corporate work. I’d even argue this extends beyond corporate America, though it’s most acute in legacy enterprises. Here’s the problem: The assumption that “this looks too good/too fast, therefore a machine wrote it” reveals a strange paradox in modern office dynamics.

Historically, responding to an email within five minutes with a structured, five-point plan signaled exceptional competence and discipline. Today, it frequently triggers an unspoken deduction: “They just dumped this into a prompt.”

We are beginning to penalize the very traits we used to reward — speed, thoroughness, and flawless formatting, simply because the barrier to producing them has dropped. The skepticism isn’t necessarily about the quality of the content; it’s a defense mechanism against perceived laziness or a lack of genuine human attention.

To counter this, the narrative needs to shift from how the output was generated to how the human directed, verified, and enhanced it. AI has elevated the baseline of daily communication. It handles the cognitive overhead of formatting, adjusting tone, and ensuring no technical details slip through the cracks. It turns a rushed mental download into an enterprise-grade draft.

The value of the output isn’t in the typing; it’s in the intent, context, and synthesis. A project charter generated by AI isn’t valuable because the LLM knows your business; it’s valuable because you knew which constraints to feed it, which variables mattered, and had the domain expertise to audit the final product before hitting send. The effort hasn’t disappeared; it has moved upstream to curation and critical thinking.

A Framework for Intentional Transparency

If the goal is to use AI transparently without triggering the “automated spam” reflex, we need a better protocol than either hiding it completely or slapping a generic “Written with AI” disclaimer on everything.

We might consider proposing a simple taxonomy or set of principles for how professionals can signal their involvement:

Approach — The “Human In The Loop” Signal

Description — Explicitly calling out the specific data or intent you provided. (e.g., “I ran our Q1 metrics through an LLM to parse the outliers, and here is what the data implies…”)

Best Used For — Deep-dive reports, technical analysis, data synthesis.

Approach — The Editorial Guardrail

Description — Using AI for structural polish but maintaining distinct personal voice, idioms, or specific institutional knowledge that an LLM couldn’t fabricate.

Best Used For — Project charters, cross-functional proposals, leadership updates.

Approach — The Show-Your-Work Principle

Description — Framing the AI as the research assistant while you remain the principal investigator. The value is explicitly derived from your final review and sign-off.

Best Used For —Complex strategy documents, technical documentation.

AI has given us the ability to generate endless noise, but our premium as professionals comes from our ability to create signal. When we use these tools transparently, pairing human domain expertise with AI’s speed to produce *sharper, more compact* work, we don’t just move faster. We make it easier for everyone else to do their jobs, too.

### It’s getting more expensive

Last week, Google wrapped up I/O and announced new Gemini models, including 3.5 Flash, plus updates to multimodal video and image generation. While performance and speeds improved, even non-flagship models are becoming much more expensive. 3.5 Flash now costs nearly 15× more per million tokens than Gemini models from just two generations ago, priced for the same token count.

These cost increases make sense when you examine the token breakdown required to run standard benchmarks. As the charts shows, many of the most capable models also consume the most tokens to produce a given output. Much of this is hidden behind the blinking “thinking” icon, but the reality is that more behind‑the‑scenes compute drives higher usage costs. Even lower‑tier users at Google are seeing tighter limits: the $20/month plan, previously only rate limited, is now token limited.

### And where does this lead?

![Figure](/images/blog/2026-05-24-dont-hide-it/image-06.png)

It’s no mystery that AI labs are exploring the UI state space to replace the software people use for daily tasks. From the new Gemini app, to Claude Cowork and Codex they are trying to shoehorn in SaaS-like capabilities like a fat guy in a small t-shirt. They aim to become the platform while selling the tokens used on it. As Bill Gates once said about Facebook Platform (it was a thing in 2007), “This isn’t a platform. A platform is where the collective sum of revenues of the participants exceeds those of the platform itself.”

It would be better if AI companies focused on inference and model capabilities instead of becoming the everything app. Growth might be slower, but it’s a more sustainable long-term plan than competing with every app while also selling them the tokens.
