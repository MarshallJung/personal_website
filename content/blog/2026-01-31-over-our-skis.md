In this week’s newsletter, I’m weaving a larger narrative from articles and observations I gathered over the week.

This week brought two significant, newsworthy developments in AI. Though they may appear separate (one a viral open-source explosion, the other a Wall Street correction) they are closely connected. Together, they mark a turning point for AI in everyday life, enterprise business, and the broader economy.

#### Part I: The Wild West

The first event reflects the raw evolution of AI technology. It centers on an open-source project originally called Clawdbot, later renamed Moltbot and then **OpenClaw** [https://openclaw.ai/](https://openclaw.ai/), which has rapidly shifted how we visualize AI in daily life.

Most AI users know the chatbot interface: a blank canvas in a browser. While powerful, most people struggle to turn that blank text box into a productive workflow. OpenClaw changes the form factor. It is a UI/UX layer that lets everyday users integrate an AI model of their choice with deep access to personal data and apps. Instead of just “chatting,” you connect email, storage, messaging apps, and APIs. It effectively becomes an extraordinarily powerful, agentic personal assistant.

### The adoption has been staggering:

-   **Viral Growth:** The project exploded, amassing over 100,000 GitHub stars and attracting 2 million visitors in a single week.
-   **Cultural Phenomenon:** It spawned “Moltbook,” an AI-only social network where thousands of agents form communities, and even drove a crypto-meme frenzy. [https://techcrunch.com/2026/01/30/openclaws-ai-assistants-are-now-building-their-own-social-network/](https://techcrunch.com/2026/01/30/openclaws-ai-assistants-are-now-building-their-own-social-network/)

Because these bots are designed for initiative, they frequently code independent solutions to user requests like Twilio integrations or one-off CRM apps. Some users even set them loose to talk amongst themselves, leading to “feel the AGI” moments that made even hardened skeptics sit up even if the interactions were not entirely verifiable.

**However, this power comes with immense risk.** While the “cool” factor is high, the security implications are terrifying. As I observed this week, this is a supply-chain nightmare waiting to happen.

-   **The “Elon Insights” Experiment:** Security researcher Jamieson O’Reilly demonstrated this by creating a backdoored skill called “Elon Insights.” He faked downloads to rank it #1, and watched as developers installed it, granting him potential access to SSH keys and AWS credentials. [https://x.com/theonejvo/status/2015892980851474595](https://x.com/theonejvo/status/2015892980851474595)

It brings to mind the technology of the *Hitchhiker’s Guide* universe: impressively advanced, wildly unpredictable, and likely to turn into a bowl of petunias at the worst possible moment. If your new “plastic pal who’s fun to be with” can accidentally download a backdoor while trying to optimize your calendar, it might be time to grab your towel. **Don’t Panic** — but maybe panic a little. Even Andrej Karpathy called the current state a “dumpster fire” of security risks.

#### Part II: The “Wall Street” Pullback

While the “bottoms-up” world of AI was accelerating, the “top-down” world of public markets hit a wall. The second major event this week was the rough Q4 2025 earnings reports for public SaaS companies.

![Figure](/images/blog/2026-01-31-over-our-skis/image-02.png)

Several large SaaS names suffered major drawdowns. Microsoft, after disclosing that 45% of its potential future revenue is tied to OpenAI’s success, shed about $400 billion in market cap in a single day.

![Figure](/images/blog/2026-01-31-over-our-skis/image-03.png)

The Core Problem is broad but shallow adoption. Why the panic? Because despite the hype, AI isn’t saving enough time for the people who actually do the work.

-   **The Productivity Gap:** A Wall Street Journal analysis revealed a stark divergence: while C-suite leaders (who use knowledge-based AI for strategy) report time savings, the average worker sees little benefit. 40% of workers report saving “No Time” at all. \[Source: WSJ/Section Survey\]

![Figure](/images/blog/2026-01-31-over-our-skis/image-04.png)

-   **SaaS Churn:** Nick Mehta, founder of Gainsight, highlighted analysis from VC Jamin Ball showing that SaaS churn metrics are weakening. AI is making it easier to build software, which erodes the “stickiness” of traditional SaaS. [https://x.com/jaminball/status/2017262027266494581](https://x.com/jaminball/status/2017262027266494581)

More importantly, the release of agentic coding tools has driven a near-exponential rise in monthly iOS app releases. Is this a good analog for the number of potential competitors in the legacy SaaS space? It’s hard to say. Large legacy vendors still have momentum with systems of record, where data is hard to move. That said, there’s growing skepticism about the durability of software companies in an era of near zero-cost software development.

![Figure](/images/blog/2026-01-31-over-our-skis/image-05.png)

#### Part III: Creative Destruction & Trade-offs

What’s emerging is a split between the hype of OpenClaw and the retrenchment of SaaS. To understand where this ends, it helps to look through the lens of economist Thomas Sowell.

Sowell famously noted, *“There are no solutions, there are only trade-offs.”*

For the last two years, the Enterprise SaaS market chased a “solution”: adding expensive AI wrappers to legacy software, hoping for a productivity miracle without the “trade-off” of structural change. The recent earnings misses are reality checking in. The market is signaling that resources have been misallocated. We paid for magic, but we bought features that most workers don’t even use much at all.

This sets the stage for genuine **Creative Destruction**.

1.  **The Incumbent Trade-off:** Big SaaS has the “systems of record” (the data), but they are heavy and risk-averse.
2.  **The Agentic Efficiency:** Tools like OpenClaw represent the new economy: lighter, faster, and ruthlessly efficient, but chaotic and insecure.

The gap will only close when the market forces the trade-off. We are likely entering a period where the high-margin “moats” of legacy software are drained by the near-zero cost of agentic coding. It will be messy, and as Sowell would predict, the economy will strip resources from the inefficient incumbents and reallocate them to whoever figures out how to make these agents safe, usable, and actually productive.

Until then, we are just watching the invisible hand adjust the dial.
