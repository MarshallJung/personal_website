### Show Your Work or WE’RE ALL GOING TO DIE!!!...or maybe not?

Long-time readers of my blog know that I occasionally talk about AI pushback from people who are plugged in online and aware of its capabilities. Even nearly a year ago, though, I encountered everyday folks, like front desk staff at car rental agencies, who were not particularly thrilled with the AI story. Now, I wouldn\x27t normally write another long thought piece on this, but this week, fear-mongering about AI\x27s effect on humanity made another meaningful whack into the public consciousness. And my job is to keep my readers grounded in the middle.

TL;DR - The builders are more worried than the public was told, and less certain than the viral posts imply.

On Tuesday, Jacob Coxon, a pre-training researcher who spent three years at OpenAI and six weeks(?) at Anthropic, resigned and posted on X.com that both labs are "racing straight to self-improving super-intelligence and gambling with our lives." He added that "the people building these systems earnestly believe that it could kill all of us by the end of the decade" and sound much calmer in on-camera interviews than they do in private at the labs.

The post that pushed the story beyond X.com was a reply from Evan Hubinger, Anthropic\x27s alignment science lead. He said, “Jacob is correct here — we really do earnestly believe AI could kill all humans. I personally think it is >10% within the next decade.” adding, "we do not yet have a plan to solve alignment for superintelligence and are clearly not on track to." Coxon then echoed this in multiple interviews across CNN, MSNOW, and other media outlets.

But calm down! This is not necessarily a horror outcome. To start, neither Anthropic employee is talking about current models. Instead, they are referring to a hypothetical future model, usually labeled as ASI (artificial superintelligence). This would be brought about by RSI (recursive self-improvement), where existing AI models help build ever more complex, increasingly inscrutable AI models to supersede themselves. All this is still hypothetical. Additionally, estimates of ~10% are personal probabilities not published numbers by any of the labs.

The difference is that these predictions reached a wider public already primed for a negative reaction. Over the past two years, major AI lab leaders have forecasted large-scale job losses and discussed misuse paths in both cyber and biology. Dario Amodei put the chance of "things go really, really badly" at about 25% and "civilizational collapse" in the 10 to 25% band, with others largely agreeing. Coxon simply voiced what several CEOs have already implied, while adding the part they usually downplay or avoid: there is no plan for the outcome their labs are actively racing toward. And some of the claimed “warning shots” already exist. The Hugging Face incident being primary.

And we also need to look at the other side. Having an artificial superintelligence by 2030 is a highly contested empirical claim (to put it mildly). Gary Marcus (GenAI sceptic at NYU) says current systems aren\x27t on a demonstrated path to the agency these extinction stories require, and other serious researchers believe the field still needs conceptual breakthroughs like world models, persistent memory, and genuine planning, not just more pre-training or fine-tuning. If that\x27s right, extinction by the end of the decade is just marketing. (There is also a political angle to this. People on X looked into the virality of Coxon\x27s original post and found it was amplified by several wealthy individual’s non-profits aligned with the anti-AI movement. [https://x.com/ParkerThayer/status/2097759699626328575?s=20](https://x.com/ParkerThayer/status/2097759699626328575?s=20) Combined with Anthropic\x27s alignment language, it starts to look less like an organic concern over AI impacts and more like an attempt at regulatory capture and market influence prior to Anthropics proposed IPO)

The intelligent middle ground is actually much narrower than the discourse suggests. Almost no informed, serious person thinks today\x27s deployed models are an extinction event, just as almost no one thinks capability is moving slower than evaluation and incident response can handle. Labor disruption, cyber offense, and biological misuse assistance are live problems. A race among a handful of private labs is simply a poor governance structure for technology the labs themselves describe as civilizationally impactful. Saying "we are racing ahead because the other guys won\x27t be careful" can be true while still being an admission of failure.

We also need to consider the basic physical reality that these fanciful doomsday scenarios tend to hand-wave away. A while back, many of the same people promoting these apocalyptic predictions wrote "ai2027," which outlined similar humanity-ending outcomes. Yet, both in that paper and in broader doomer discourse, they never actually show their work.

How are models that depend on extraordinarily large, astronomically expensive compute supposed to escape their confines and take over the world\x27s digital systems? Most of those systems are either air-gapped or so clunkily installed that they hardly present an attack surface at all. Anyone who visits middle America and talks with an average 5,000-person enterprise would see that releasing an AI agent into their tech stack would have almost zero effect on daily operations. Their systems are simply too manual, disconnected, and old-school to trigger a global meltdown.

If people truly believe an apocalyptic scenario is coming, they need to lay out precisely how it would happen and show their work. Rather most of their stories are just that…stories. There has been (so far) absolutely no concrete evidence of the kind of capabilities that these folks claim will bring about the calamities they describe. One might even say that Von Neumann was correct when he said about Oppenheimer\x27s performative angst over the atomic bomb: “Some people profess guilt to claim credit for the sin.”

All that said, human extinction isn\x27t the base case for this decade but damaging outcomes are still possible. Treating it as science fiction after the people training the models have said otherwise is a mistake, though a statistically small one at this point in time. The grounded view is that AI will reshape power, work, and security in uneven ways, and racing toward self-improving AI systems without a published control plan is reckless, even if humans manage to survive it. If the labs truly do believe that they need to slow down and “pace the frontier” then by all means go ahead and do it. Anthropic and OpenAI are the frontier. If they truly, deeply believe in the existential threat AI poses then they should set the example and pause. If they don’t, then perhaps, that is all we really need to know.

### A $1,000,000,000,000 TAM

You will likely hear the word "ontology" much more often in the enterprise space moving forward. Popularized by Palantir, an ontology describes the relationships between data, workflows, and actions, rather than acting as traditional master data management.

Mapping these relationships across a large enterprise is extraordinarily difficult. Palantir\x27s pitch has always been that moving your entire company onto their Foundry platform gives you "one ring to rule them all," solving the problem directly. In reality, most organizations will not do that. Because of this, I have been approached by companies ranging from SAP and Salesforce to Accenture, Deloitte, and Databricks, all pitching ontological solutions to handle the complex internal workflows at Valvoline Global Operations.

This is a tough, but essential problem to solve. For enterprise intelligence to become truly AI-first, allowing senior leaders to ask simple natural language queries and receive verifiable, up-to-date answers from core systems of record, organizations need an ontological layer. That layer is what understands the query, navigates the underlying data relationships, and surfaces accurate answers in real time.

[https://academy.dair.ai/papers/procedural-graphs-self-evolving-execution-structures-for-llm-agents-2609.09153](https://academy.dair.ai/papers/procedural-graphs-self-evolving-execution-structures-for-llm-agents-2609.09153)

This paper breaks down why traditional agent interactions on company systems often fall apart. Most agents choose actions by generating over an accumulating history, leaving procedural knowledge implicit in the context. As trajectories grow longer, they tend to lose track of objectives, call tools out of order, and repeat failed actions.

To fix this, a procedural graph makes that knowledge explicit by storing facts as entity-relation-entity triplets. Although the paper doesn\x27t propose a specific tech stack, it would be great to see someone implement this concept using something like a Neo4j database so it can integrate smoothly with existing AI rollouts and harnesses.
