Not to beat a dead horse, but the risks, ethics and misunderstandings of AI continue to be something that I discuss with folks on an increasing basis and especially after the recent Google Gemini issues. I think there are some more interesting thoughts that can be added to last week’s paragraphs. Occasionally one of my dear readers will write to me offering some insight, observation or correction to something that I post in this weekly newsletter. It is one astute insight that I received last week that I think bears some additional introspection. He writes, *“The Twitter kerfuffle was unfortunately also a lot of flawed arguments that were piled on. For starters, evaluating a creative imagination tool for factual accuracy.”* This is an accurate observation and I agree that image generation tools should not be held to 100% historical or factual accuracy. Transformer based models in general should not be held to those standards. There are exceptions like code generation tools, but that’s a different discussion. Full transparency on training data selection biases, fine tuning/HLF choices and system prompting would go a LONG way to making the outputs acceptable even if they are wildly wrong. I talked about trust, because as these models become more human-like, the need for users to trust them becomes even more important. I can engage with someone that has massively different perspectives in life if they are authentic, truthful, and kind in their communication with me. Being guarded, recalcitrant or unhelpful is grounds for mistrust. I see no difference for LLMs. The first tech major to follow this path will win this battle even if there are better models (benchmarking) elsewhere.

However as the week went on I thought a bit more about why these big language models are behaving in the way they are. ChatGPT and now Meta ([https://www.axios.com/2024/03/01/meta-ai-google-gemini-black-founding-fathers](https://www.axios.com/2024/03/01/meta-ai-google-gemini-black-founding-fathers)) make the same mistakes as Gemini, though it looks like Google has been the one to catch the brunt of the blowback. As pedantic as it seems, the problem lies in the data itself. Yes, there can be additional human inputs that affect the model in undesirable ways as I stated above, but ultimately it’s the data used for these models. Based on some basic research I’m sure could be better, we are creating about 130 zettabytes of data in 2023/2024 and that will only continue to grow over time. Most of the world’s data is new. We have a recency bias issue here.

![Figure](/images/blog/2024-03-04-feedback/image-01.jpg)

So when Google grabs all the considerable amount of information it has available to train Gemini, we can be certain that a large percentage of that data will be relatively recent. Let’s use an example. That one Gemini gaffe where Elon Musk is found to be comparable to Adolf Hitler. Leaving aside the fact that the question in general is a terrible one to ask a generative AI, the tool itself will have a great deal more recent information about Musk, some good but mostly bad due to the negativity effect of human psychology. Thus when asked to compare a historical figure that is inarguably bad with a current person that quite likely has vastly more negative words written about them, it’s not a surprise that we would see the algorithm try and draw an equivalence. Gemini doesn’t have ethical understanding, it simply works off the data in its training set. Keeping this in mind will allow anyone to first ask the right questions, but also to be aware of the model’s outputs and the reasons for the observed behavior.

Backing up a bit, I think it’s also important to understand the data conundrum from the perspective of Google, or any other big tech company that is hoping to develop and monetize a massive multi-modal general AI model. It is in effect a monkey’s paw, and I do wonder if Google leadership is having these discussions at the moment. Given the sheer volume of data needed to build the AI, there is no practical way to QC the data. You can try and fine tune a few edge cases, or sys prompt out the most egregious issues, but you’ll never catch everything and you’ll always piss off half the folks no matter what. Every horrible response generated will garner groans and blowback since no one understands how the tool works and why it gives bad answers sometimes. Gemini will bring them notoriety, but in the worst possible way. Google needed to compete in the generative AI space, but I bet they wish they hadn’t.

### Teach someone to fish?

[https://github.com/BatsResearch/bonito](https://github.com/BatsResearch/bonito)

Most open source LLMs are pre-trained on generic web data, meant to cover a wide range of use cases. It’s a one-size-fits-all approach. However, most folks want to use these models in more specific industry applications. Self supervision, typically next word prediction, is often used to adapt LLMs to the target domain. But this approach requires an enormous amount of training to achieve strong performance. Also, self supervision can undo the benefits of instruction tuning. This code creates synthetic instruction tuning data. They train on a new dataset by remixing existing instruction tuning datasets into meta-templates, which, in turn, generate the training examples. Using the model to generate synthetic tasks and adapt pretrained and instruction tuned language models on three target tasks across seven datasets. I think this is an interesting option for working with a 3D or multimodal dataset in design that may not have significant publicly available data and remains flexible enough to be applied to new examples.
