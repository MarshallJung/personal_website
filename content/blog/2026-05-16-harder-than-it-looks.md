### It’s Harder than it Looks

### [**https://x.com/twistartups/status/2053996739879665930?s=20**](https://x.com/twistartups/status/2053996739879665930?s=20)

[**https://www.theinformation.com/briefings/google-hire-hundreds-engineers-help-customers-adopt-ai**](https://www.theinformation.com/briefings/google-hire-hundreds-engineers-help-customers-adopt-ai)

Longtime readers may recall many months ago when I coined “The Carbon Bottleneck,” noting that the slow adoption of AI isn’t due to models or deployment tech, it’s people and processes(data!!!) within organizations that hinder uptake of the AI revolution. Salesforce CEO Marc Benioff even referred to this in the latest episode of the All-In podcast ([https://youtu.be/jJRAvZNGUvI?si=jEyrONrgLIracGx7](https://youtu.be/jJRAvZNGUvI?si=jEyrONrgLIracGx7)) I’m often tired of being right (humor, I’m never tired of it), but it seems at least two major frontier AI labs are now tackling this issue. OpenAI announced a deployment company (“DeployCo”), and Google publicly said it will hire hundreds of engineers to deploy AI within target organizations. These forward-deployed engineers follow Palantir’s model, where tool complexity makes adoption difficult for traditional enterprise structures.

How likely are these strategies to succeed? In the short to medium term (1–2 years), they’re likely to accelerate adoption and create real competitive differentiation. Palantir has already proved the FDE model works at scale for complex data and AI deployments, and PE/consultancy backing gives OpenAI and Google instant distribution across thousands of portfolio companies.

Longer term, there are risks. Services businesses scale linearly with headcount, high salaries, and retention wars, while AI model companies are priced for exponential software margins. Skeptics question whether OpenAI’s or Google’s valuations hold if a large share of revenue becomes body-shop consulting. There’s also domain depth versus model expertise: most forward deployed engineers are excellent technically but lack deep vertical and historical judgment for every use case. Vendor lock-in is a concern, and FDEs can leave process debt unless knowledge transfer is deliberate. Perhaps biggest of all are cultural and organizational bottlenecks. Accountability and redesign incentives often require top-down organizational change, not just engineers.

I’m sharing this because it matters for companies already hiring in-house AI talent. In my experience, highly paid consultants or FDEs often get leadership’s attention, even when internal teams have said the same thing for months. And yes, CYA is an unspoken, if expensive, feature of the outside consultant. I’m aware, thank you.

But if I, an average but enthusiastic AI practitioner, can anticipate many of these developments before the media or other AI companies, then your in-house experts can too. Listen to them. Give them the authority to drive meaningful AI change. You don’t need to spend hundreds of thousands — or millions — on external consultants to confirm what you already know. It just takes courage to act.

(P.S. — As @emollick on X said, “You’ll know AI labs truly believe in artificial superintelligence when they disband their new “consulting” groups. As long as people are needed to figure out AI’s usefulness and handle organizational change and systems integration, most corporate jobs seem safe.” He’s right. As AI models get “smarter,” implementing them is becoming more technical. There was a time when people imagined we’d just talk to our computers in the enterprise and work would happen. Instead, the “agentic age” is pushing us back to systems engineering fundamentals. We don’t need fewer engineers, we need an army to build the digital plumbing. CLIs, model context protocols, and APIs that let an agent talk to a 20-year-old legacy database. You can’t automate solid, scalable system architecture. Rising complexity is forcing a hard pivot in IT spending. Compute is now treated like a raw utility, with teams meticulously budgeting tokens against a hierarchy of needs. Prioritizing high-value back-office rot ignored for decades. The all-in-one platform is under threat because agents don’t need a slick UI; they need access to a system’s guts. Enterprises now treat headless capability as a deal-breaker. Vendors must make it technically and economically easy for third-party agents to interact with their data. Interoperability is back in vogue. Maybe the “agentic age” isn’t about promised ease of use, but about the hard technical work of finally fixing the fragmented systems that have held us back since the 1990s.)

### Useful Stuff

Here are a few ideas to post this week to add to the toolbox and help curious folks learn more about AI systems while practicing their skills.

[https://x.com/0xCodez/status/2054235793250554086?s=20](https://x.com/0xCodez/status/2054235793250554086?s=20) — Here’s a 30-minute video from Anthropic on building an agent with memory. Beyond a custom GPT or basic Microsoft Copilot agents. A common request in my role at Valvoline Global Operations is creating agents with real-time understanding of organizational data (HR, supply chain, etc.). Teams need agents that aren’t static, don’t require frequent updates, and don’t rely on humans to track data coverage — so answers remain trustworthy. This is a clear, simple example of how to do that.

[https://github.com/anthropics/claude-for-legal](https://github.com/anthropics/claude-for-legal) — Here’s a repository from Anthropic with skills, data connectors, and agent descriptions for common legal workflows — covering in‑house commercial, privacy, product, corporate, employment litigation, regulatory, AI governance, IP, and legal education. It’s a strong starting point and fully customizable for your organization, contracts, or specific applications.

[https://x.com/trq212/status/2052809885763747935](https://x.com/trq212/status/2052809885763747935) — Finding effective ways to interact with LLMs is constantly evolving. Information density is crucial, and token efficiency matters. In some cases, code is most efficient; often, plain text or Markdown best distills English interaction. Many people are visual learners, though, and HTML can enrich communication for them. This article offers an interesting case for using LLMs with HTML on the front end to improve information transfer and user answers.

[https://huggingscience.co/](https://huggingscience.co/) — Open source data and models will be a vital part of enterprise AI. More on that in a future newsletter. For now, Hugging Face has released a collection of science datasets and enabled fine-tuning infra on top of state-of-the-art open-source models, so scientists and R&D teams can build tailored models for tough problems. I haven’t reviewed all the datasets yet, but there may be strong opportunities relevant to your work. Take a look.

### AI in the actual real world

[https://thinkingmachines.ai/blog/interaction-models/](https://thinkingmachines.ai/blog/interaction-models/) — Thinking Machines, founded by former OpenAI exiles, is taking a long-term, broad approach to AI. Think of the early wearable devices (pocket clips or necklaces) that recorded daily interactions via cameras and audio to give an AI deeply personalized context. That vision is hard to execute, but Thinking Machines has released an initial version.

They call these “interaction models”: multimodal, near real-time AIs designed to collaborate like people with messaging, talking, listening, seeing, showing, and interjecting as needed. I believe this is the future of AI, though it may not yet have broad enterprise applications. Let’s keep an eye on it; they’ve secured significant funding.

### Creative Writing

I know many of you didn’t love my dialogue-based storytelling last week. But exercising the shower-thought to writing pipeline is important, so here’s a piece about a phenomenon I experience daily. I hope you enjoy it.

The Case of the Quantum Kibble

Observe the facts of the matter. I possess four felines. I love them, which is fortunate, because otherwise, they are just tiny, unemployed roommates who shed on my mysteries. I feed them on a strict, frequent schedule. Because if you do not feed a cat on schedule, the cat becomes an alarm clock that bites you.

Each kitten has its own designated food vessel. Four cats, four bowls. If one cat uses another’s bowl, it is no longer dinner, it is a hostile takeover with whiskers.

Now, three of these creatures adhere to exquisite, predictable habit. But the fourth is a rogue agent. An anarchist with cute mittens. He insists on dining wherever the spirit moves him. As his devoted human, I must deliver the meal to his location.

The clues are limited. He only eats in one of three specific places. Statistically speaking, I have a one-in-three chance of finding his dining parlor on the first attempt. But statistics are fake. They are just numbers lying to you about your bad luck. I always check the wrong spot first. I am somehow consistently batting zero-for-three on a three-option multiple-choice quiz.

If I deduce he is at the cat tree, he is by the bathroom. If I run to the bathroom, he is by the window. If I check all three simultaneously, he is somehow on the ceiling.

The conclusion is inescapable. When you have eliminated the impossible, whatever remains, however absurd, must be that a cat’s aura bends the very fabric of the cosmos. Feline proximity fundamentally undermines basic arithmetic. It’s like one plus one equals me holding a can of kibble in the dark, looking like an idiot. The bowl exists in a state of quantum superposition. It is strictly located wherever my eyeballs are not.

We desperately require a new mathematical principle. I shall pen a monograph upon it. I think I will call it: The Cat is Here, But the Bowl is… Wait, Where is the Bowl?
