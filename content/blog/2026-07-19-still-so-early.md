That 2.2% figure sparked a look into the data. A few additional metrics worth considering: (numbers seem a little mismatched, but I don’t know survey methods)

A mere 0.2% of American households allocate $100+ monthly to AI services.  
Just 1% of total adults in the U.S. maintain a paid subscription to Claude maybe 1.5% with OpenAI.  
Only 4% of domestic adults report nearly continuous AI engagement.  
Total Claude adoption among U.S. adults sits at roughly 6% of the roughly 2.2%.  
Merely 4.5% have utilized an AI agent for autonomous task completion.  
Only 8.3% of the workforce feels AI enables previously impossible workflows.

All say that we are still very, very early.

### P-Doom

It’s vital for anyone with strong views, especially in technical fields, to keep an open mind and read those who strongly disagree. In AI, I try to balance the futuristic, utopian predictions with the doomer perspective that warns of collapse or severe economic disruption.

Ed Zitron, a financial commentator and prolific journalist, stands out as a leading AI critic, with strong sources inside many Silicon Valley venture capital and tech firms. In his latest newsletter, there’s an early paragraph that summarizes his view on AI’s current impact in the enterprise market.

*“The AI bubble isn’t a result of any actual return on investment — whether that be in purely monetary terms, like revenue or profitability , productivity gains, or anything tangible or measurable. Rather, it’s an episode of cult-like psychosis that infected the brains of some of the most powerful and wealthy individuals and institutions, where the powerful mythology of a company inspired — and been used to inspire — the greatest capital misallocation in history.” — Ed Zitron*

I agree with parts of Ed’s take on the AI investment supercycle. There is real misallocation, and a reckoning is likely as apps and claims fail to deliver ROI, with potential knock-on effects in the U.S. and globally. Still I think AI critics are making a category error when it comes to understanding AI as “software”.

Ed and his cohort often overlook that clear, demonstrable AI ROIs are hard to capture in neat, reportable financial metrics. Much of the disagreement between AI enthusiasts and naysayers stems from the lack of a repeatable, easily documentable set of AI improvements that apply universally across enterprises. Both sides lean on anecdotes.

Given that, I can’t outright reject Ed’s view. My belief is that AI’s most tangible impact so far has been in hyper-personalized environments. Here are a couple of observations from my role as Senior Director of Global AI at Valvoline Global Operations. I use a two-pronged strategy: broad AI enablement is hard to measure for ROI across thousands of employees, so while we roll out AI to each employee we also prioritize targeted moonshot projects that are more visible to leadership and help secure support to scale AI across the organization. In my experience (likely shared by others in similar roles) demand is intense: employees across functions have real, specific use cases, from invoice processing and customer email management to specialized technical workflows. Nearly everyone is eager to use AI or get more access to it.

Sure, much AI use may be superfluous or redundant, and there’s an ongoing cultural effort to maintain high-quality, tasteful output. The battle against organizational “slopification” will continue for the foreseeable future. Still, I’ve seen enough examples of employees doing interesting, useful work with AI that have driven overall efficiency gains.

AI adoption is often misunderstood by critics like Ed Zitron who aren’t directly involved with how average companies are using it today. Across most organizations, interest and enthusiasm to learn and use AI are growing; employees aren’t resisting out of job fear. With the right culture and rollout, they see AI as a way to become better employees and take on more responsibility, not as a threat.

This diffuse, highly individualized adoption isn’t easy to measure and rarely reaches the naysayers’ information channels. I have to push back, because company AI consumption and spend aren’t declining as they predict. The expansion is often invisible to those anchored in older biz-tech models, where technology is a named product with a fixed feature set, distributed uniformly across companies. Traditional software growth narratives don’t match how AI is spreading inside organizations today.

To close this section by again aiming for middle ground: I’m not claiming AI will be adopted fast enough to offset AI companies’ current overinvestment. In my experience, that’s unlikely. This looks like a 3–5 year process, not an 18-month one. Will companies like OpenAI and Anthropic fail as startups? The probability is yes, with another company likely picking up the torch. Creative destruction and the reallocation of malinvestment are part of the capitalistic process. Still, the technology is here to stay, and its maturation and distribution will differ significantly from traditional software. I hope this clarifies how I see AI’s macroeconomic path, and I welcome pushback and perspectives on what I might be missing.

### Open Source Attacks

This past week saw two notable open-source releases. Kimi K3 from MoonshotAI in China and Inkling from Thinking Machines in the United States, he first truly interesting and meaningful U.S. open-source model in a while. (I’m not counting Meta here, as their recent releases have fallen short of being interesting, applicable, or competitive with frontier models.)

I haven’t seen benchmarks for Inkling yet; we’ll cover those in a future newsletter, along with what they might mean for open source in the US. As for Kimi K3, it ranked first on frontend benchmarks and scored high on other tasks in the coding/reasoning space as well. However, because it was trained on older hardware and lacks some proprietary efficiency methodologies that the US closed source labs possess, it’s notably inefficient in token usage.

Reduced margin percentages at the base model layer effectively translate into greater dollar margins across every segment of the infrastructure stack. A true gift for the software industry. This shift occurs when open-source options like K3 reach the frontier, or when vertically integrated giants such as Meta, SpaceX, or Google maintain pace. In both scenarios, the model layer’s profitability shrinks because these integrated firms are indifferent to which specific bucket generates their returns. This dynamic explains the immense pressure on OpenAI and Anthropic whenever Google achieves parity; it’s also why the arrival of Grok 4.5 and Muse 1.1 carries as much weight as the release of Kimi K3.

Open-source models, and task-specific fine-tunes, will only grow in importance, whether run on your own infrastructure or in the cloud. I’m still waiting for a breakthrough in the model routing and middleware layer, but in a couple of months we’ll probably be spoiled for choice.
