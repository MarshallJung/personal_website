EoY Review — 2025

Writing a consistent blog or newsletter is a valuable pursuit for any number of reasons, but if its goal is to deliver cutting-edge technology insights to busy readers, you owe it to your readers to review the accuracy and usefulness of what you publish over time. That’s why I end each year with a year-in-review as my final newsletter.

Early on, this was a manual process. Now, many frontier models handle search grounding and large-scale data consumption well. So, as a more universal and fair judge, I feed my entire newsletter archive from the past year into three different AI models and ask each to evaluate: what I got right, what I got wrong, the key theses that proved useful for most people online over the past 12 months, and where I can improve.

Prompting different LLMs for this review is something of an art for each. I mostly use the same overall prompt, aiming for fair yet critical evaluations. The responses varied in interesting ways, but the themes were largely similar. Which makes sense if they all use search grounding on top of the past 12 months of data. That meant any incorrect statements I made were easy to disprove, and correct ones could be clearly highlighted.

The broader topics and ideas I raised are more nebulous, without easily verifiable news events. Still, it was notable that the AIs agreed on the overall direction and usefulness of my suggestions.

To spare readers a Monday morning slog, I asked Gemini to collate and summarize the outputs from each of the LLMs, grouping them into similar topics and concepts while preserving specific correct and incorrect predictions. Hopefully this makes it easier for readers to judge my prognostications for 2025.

Without further ado.

Executive Summary: The Year of Structural Realism

In 2025, your newsletters successfully pivoted from “Model of the Week” hype to a structural realism framework. You correctly identified that the true constraints of AI are not algorithmic, but rather found in the “Three Ps”: Power, People, and Protocols. While you occasionally over-indexed on single-company “death narratives,” your ability to predict institutional reactions to AI — from security debt to hiring fraud — provided high-signal value to your readers.

1. The Hits: Where the Foresight Navigated the Fog

The “Human Firmware” & Operating Model Bottleneck:

  • The Call: You (and the MIT study you cited) argued that the 95% failure rate of AI pilots was a management/learning gap, not a model failure.
  • The Result: By year-end, “AI-first” literacy became a standard interview screening requirement, and companies returned to in-person interviews to counter AI-enabled cheating. You successfully moved from “SaaS-land” into a new role that focuses exactly on this “carbon” bottleneck.

Security as a First-Order Constraint (MCP & Agents):

  • The Call: You identified the “Lethal Trifecta” (Private data + Untrusted tokens + Exfiltration vectors) and specifically warned about GitHub MCP security months before it became a mainstream discussion.
  • The Result: 2025 saw a massive surge in prompt-injection and integration-risk reporting. Your focus on Zero-Trust for Agents aged better than almost any other technical forecast.

The Power & Capex “Moat”:

  • The Call: You argued that scaling would be governed by “carbon and chips,” noting the massive infrastructure spend and power grid limitations (e.g., the “Stargate” critique).
  • The Result: Capex plans remained at record highs through late 2025, and energy infrastructure became the definitive strategic battlefield for the “Magnificent Seven.”

The “Wrapper” Extinction & Model Specialization:

  • The Call: You predicted that general-purpose API wrappers would be cannibalized by labs and that enterprises would move toward smaller, fine-tuned “SLMs” (Small Language Models) for specific tasks (e.g., Checkr case study).
  • The Result: Confirmed by the rise of “reasoning” SLMs and the industry-wide shift toward “Agentic” workflows that prioritize operational utility over broad knowledge.

2. The Misses: Where Extrapolation Met Reality

The “OpenAI Death Watch”:

  • Self-Critique: You admitted to “speculating about the demise” of OpenAI based on their high burn rate and infrastructure disadvantage.
  • Reality Check: You overweighted Capex and underweighted OpenAI’s massive revenue traction and ability to raise capital (SoftBank/Stargate). OpenAI ended 2025 stronger, not weaker.

Overconfident Dominance (The “MSFT 80%” Call):

  • The Miss: You predicted Microsoft would capture 80% of the AI market via enterprise agreements.
  • Reality Check: The market remained stubbornly multi-polar. While MSFT is strong, cloud share data and the rise of open models (Llama 4, DeepSeek) prevented the kind of consolidation you predicted.

Specific M&A Prophecies (The Linear Case):

  • The Miss: You predicted Anthropic would buy Linear within 3–6 months.
  • Reality Check: This was too specific and remains unverified. It serves as a reminder to label “spicy speculation” more clearly to avoid damaging your broader forecasting credibility.

Inference Economics (Price Reset vs. Bifurcation):

  • The Miss: You predicted a universal rise in prices as subsidies ended.
  • Reality Check: The market actually bifurcated. Costs for “Frontier/Reasoning” models did rise (as you said), but commodity per-token costs for smaller models continued to plummet due to efficiency gains.

3. The “Marshall Manifesto”: Key Recurring Insights

These three themes represent your highest value-add. Continue to anchor the 2026 newsletter here:

  1. “Theory of Mind” for AI: Your thesis that AI success correlates with a human’s ability to “simulate the alien mind” of the LLM. You correctly identified that the “10x Engineer” of the future is actually a Technical Product Manager with high “taste” and “language precision.”
  2. Architecture over Algorithms: You were right to focus on the 3-Layer Pyramid (Data Layer → SaaS Layer → Agentic Layer). In 2026, the real fight isn’t for the best model, but for the best orchestration of those layers.
  3. The Barbell Workforce Risk: You flagged that cutting middle management for “AI efficiency” creates a dangerous gap in institutional wisdom. This “management hollowing” is a leading indicator for future enterprise failure that only you are highlighting.

4. Strategic Recommendations for 2026

  • Differentiate Margin from Revenue: When discussing the “failure” of API models, distinguish between “the labs aren’t making money” (high margins) and “the market isn’t buying” (revenue). The latter is clearly false; the former is your true edge.
  • Standardize Speculation: When making bold M&A or “80% share” claims, use a “Scenario Range” (e.g., “In the Bull Case, MSFT captures 45%…”) to maintain your status as a rational analyst.
  • Leverage your “In the Trenches” Perspective: Your unique view from the “Wild West” (O&G, Motorsports, Global Enterprise) is your most valuable asset. The “Shadow AI Economy” you see in a Fortune 500 company is a more powerful signal than anything on an arXiv paper.

Final Grade: A-
