The 2026 Scorecard: Boring is the New Bullish

Continuing the newsletter tradition, and following last week’s review of my 2025 prognostication accuracy, this week leads with predictions for 2026. AI feedback on last week’s 2025 review suggested my predictions should be more quantifiable to be taken seriously. I’ve asked Gemini to provide a short blurb (the “How to Verify” below) on how to check each prediction at year’s end. This could be useful enough that I’ll drop the entire newsletter into a prompt at the end of 2026 and ask the model to apply those verification methodologies… Hmmmm

Before we begin, a quick note: these are just my predictions based on what I know. Some may already be true,I just haven’t heard about them yet so as the kids say “Don’t @ me bro.” My only goal is to be specific enough to be verifiable, but broad enough to be actionable for anyone navigating the inertia of a large enterprise. And of course I hope that my readers get a bit of value as well.

The Context: The Hangover After the Party
Before we get to the scorecard, let’s summarize this year past. 2025 was a paradox. It was an amazing year for tooling. Frontier models from Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google made code generation so cheap that we saw a Cambrian explosion of new software. I’d wager more startups were launched in the last 12 months than in the previous 20 years combined. Most will fail, of course, but that is the power of lowered barriers to entry; more actualized ideas is never a bad thing.

However, despite the noise in the startup ecosystem, AI has not yet breached the fortress of the large-scale enterprise. Legacy workflows, fossilized SaaS ecosystems, and organizational inertia are incredibly resistant to change.

The Outlook: I expect 2026 to be a volatile, sideways year. The early adopters are saturated. The next tranche of revenue relies on enterprise deployments that move at the speed of procurement audits, not Twitter demos. The bottom lines of the big AI labs will feel this friction, leading to a winnowing of the field.

  • The Hot Take: Yes, despite the market slapping me in the face for the second year in a row, I am doubling down on my prediction: OpenAI will face a structural reckoning this year (split, sale, down round, or acquisition). The economics of scaling are colliding with the reality of enterprise adoption rates.

With that cheery macro outlook established, here are my verifiable, falsifiable predictions for 2026.

1. The Rise of the “No” Layer (Security & Identity)

The “Enterprise AI Control Plane” is becoming the actual product. We can no longer treat LLMs as trusted employees; we must treat them as untrusted microservices.

  • The Prediction: By year-end, the “AI Firewall” becomes a standard SKU. At least 6 major security vendors will have GA offerings explicitly positioned as AI Gateways. These won’t just be API wrappers. They will offer deep inspection for OWASP-style risks: prompt injection, PII redaction, and protection against excessive agency.
  • The Prediction: “Agent Identity” enters the IAM lexicon. Agents are touching production systems, which means they need badges. At least 2 top-tier IAM/PAM vendors (think Okta) will market dedicated capabilities for non-human agent identities (scoped permissions, Just-in-Time access).
  • How to Verify: Check vendor release notes, Gartner categories, and Fortune 500 job postings for “Agent Identity” roles.

2. The Architecture of Parsimony (Routing & Distillation)

We are done using a PhD-level model to summarize a calendar invite. 2026 is the year of Model Routing — dynamic traffic control based on cost, latency, and risk.

  • The Prediction: Policy-based routing becomes a default platform feature, not a bespoke engineering hack. At least 3 major enterprise AI platforms will offer native routing (e.g., “If query complexity < X, send to small model; else, send to Big”) integrated with logging.
  • The Prediction: The “Teacher/Student” architecture dominates. At least 2 hyperscalers will productize managed distillation workflows. We will see a flood of case studies where small models handle the critical path, and large models are relegated to edge cases and offline evaluation.
  • How to Verify: Platform documentation features and architecture diagrams in public enterprise case studies.

3. The End of the “All-You-Can-Eat” Token Buffet

The economics of inference are volatile. The “unlimited” plans of the past few years are commercially fragile when users start automating complex agentic loops.

  • The Prediction: Credit-based pricing spreads to the enterprise. At least 3 major copilot/agent platforms will introduce usage caps or “advanced reasoning packs” sold via credits.
  • The Prediction: Reserved Capacity becomes the standard for serious players. At least 2 frontier model providers will offer committed throughput (tokens-per-minute guarantees) with SLA-like language. You want predictable performance? You have to pay for the reserved instance.
  • How to Verify: Pricing pages, licensing guides, and earnings call commentary regarding “committed capacity.”

4. The “Bounded Agency” Reality Check

Despite the hype about autonomous agents replacing entire departments, the friction of reality: sec/reg compliance, error rates, and human anxiety will act as a governor.

  • The Prediction: Bounded Agency remains the norm. Major analyst surveys (McKinsey/Deloitte) will show that fewer than 20% of organizations have scaled agentic systems across 3+ business functions. Scaled deployments will stay concentrated in IT and Knowledge Management, where the blast radius of an error is manageable.
  • How to Verify: Compare 2026 analyst survey results against 2025 baselines regarding “agent scaling.”

Final Thoughts:
I’ll let my predictions stand for now, but before we go I want to get “meta” and discuss why AI may be the most deterministic predictor of the future among general technologies currently devised by humankind. LLMs weren’t designed as oracles, yet if we accept that, in any sequence of events, the most probable outcome tends to occur, then a probabilistic approach to our timeline makes sense. With frontier AI labs and large language models trained on the preponderance of internet data and now augmented by real-time information via web grounding, we can generate predictions for events in domains where sufficient information exists for the model to perform the most probable sequential vector search. (And yes I’m aware that real-time web grounding isn’t specifically “training data”, however it seems likely that some representation of real-time grounding would at least be contextual information for model response.) I’m quite sure that I’m not the first to consider this concept, but it’s worth exploring. And since I love/live in the practical world I’m quite sure there are likely everyday practical enterprise applications as well, which we can address later.
