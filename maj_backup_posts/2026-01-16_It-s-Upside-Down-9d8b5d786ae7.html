Why “Moonshots” Are Actually the Low-Hanging Fruit

I thought my job was to teach the workforce to fish. It turns out, the best way to start is to build a better fishing boat.

When I stepped into the role of Global Head of AI, I brought a standard playbook with me. It was a logical, three-horizon roadmap widely accepted in the industry:

  1. Short-term: Education. Identify enthusiasts, upskill the broader workforce, and foster a culture of curious generality.
  2. Medium-term: Process integration.
  3. Long-term: The “Moonshots.” Building fine-tuned expert models, like a co-scientist for R&D or hyper-personalized marketing workflows.

It seemed obvious. You have to walk before you can run. You need a data-literate culture before you can deploy complex models.

I had it backwards.

After spending time back in the trenches of a traditional enterprise, I’ve realized that starting with broad upskilling puts the cart before the horse. The moonshots, those high-tech, complex builds I pushed to the back of the roadmap, are surprisingly accessible. More importantly, they are the necessary spark to ignite the cultural change we want to see.

Here is why I’m flipping the roadmap.

Respecting the Cognitive Load (The Curiosity Gap)

My initial strategy assumed that if we provided the tools and the training, our workforce would naturally pivot to experimentation.

However, I realized that this approach underestimates the demands we already place on our teams. Our scientists, engineers, and logistics experts are hired for their deep, vertical expertise. They are focused on executing high-stakes tasks with precision. Asking them to pause that work to become “AI explorers”…to develop a new, generalized muscle for prompt engineering and model interaction, is a significant request.

It isn’t that our teams lack the capacity for curiosity or growth. Far from it. It’s that asking them to drive the innovation curve while doing their day jobs creates friction. It places the burden of proof on the employee.

Engineering constraints are often easier to solve than attention constraints. A small, dedicated AI team can be curious on demand. We can build the solution for the workforce, rather than asking the workforce to build it for themselves.

Tangible vs. Abstract Value

When we start with upskilling, we are selling an abstraction. We tell leadership: “If we train 500 people, they might each save 30 minutes a week.”

This is mathematically sound but emotionally hollow. It is difficult for a busy professional to visualize how a chat bot changes their specific reality.

Compare that to a Moonshot.

  • Abstract: “Let’s improve R&D efficiency by 10% through better prompt engineering training.”
  • Tangible: “Here is a fine-tuned ‘Synthetic Chemist’ model. It has ingested every formula we’ve ever patented. You can ask it to predict the stability of a new mixture, and it will give you an answer in seconds.”

In an operations-heavy business, tangible wins every time. A specific tool that solves a specific headache creates immediate political capital. It moves the conversation from “What is AI?” to “When can I have that?”

The Trojan Horse Effect

This brings me to the most important realization: The Moonshot is not the destination; it is the vehicle for education.

I used to think education had to precede execution. Now I see that execution is education.

When we deploy a high-value tool — like a hyper-personalized marketing workflow, we aren’t just solving a business problem. We are planting a “Trojan Horse” for AI adoption. Once the marketing team sees the model generate better copy in a fraction of the time, the fear of AI is replaced by the utility of the tool. They don’t need to be cajoled into learning how it works. They demand to know how to steer it.

By delivering the moonshot first, we create a vacuum that pulls education along behind it. We don’t have to push training on anyone; the successful tool creates the pull.

The New Roadmap

If you are leading AI in a non-tech enterprise, my advice is this: Don’t get bogged down trying to turn every accountant and engineer into a prompt engineer on day one. That is the long game.

Instead, respect their time by giving them something that works. Rally your small team of experts and ship a moonshot. Build the tool that makes the R&D team say, “Wow.” Delivery creates belief faster than a workshop ever will.

Navel Gazing

A I promised a few weeks ago I am following up on my meta concept of AI as “The Oracle”. It took me a few weeks to get the words right but here is something more concrete:

The Oracle in the GPU: Why AI is the End of “I Didn’t See That Coming”

In one of my previous newsletters, I posed a question that is less about technology and more about the physics of existence: If LLMs are just “next token predictors,” and reality is just a sequence of events, are we accidentally building a deterministic oracle?

Pierre-Simon Laplace once theorized that an intellect knowing the position and momentum of every atom in the universe could perfectly predict the future. We called this “Laplace’s Demon.” We never built it because we couldn’t track every atom.

But it turns out, we don’t need to track atoms. We just need to track context.

Here is why the “Probabilistic Timeline” is the most disruptive concept of the decade, and how it challenges our fundamental views on existence:

The Death of the “Black Swan” Nassim Taleb defined Black Swans as unpredictable, high-impact events. But are they truly unpredictable, or did we just lack the compute to see the pattern? If an AI, trained on the sum total of human history and grounded in real-time web data, can perform a sequential vector search on “Global Economic Stability,” it might see the crash coming six months before the market does. There are no surprises anymore. There is only insufficient context windows. “Unprecedented” is just a word we use to excuse our lack of data processing power.

The Digital Collective Unconscious (Jung via GPU) Carl Jung (no relation) believed in a “collective unconscious”, structures of the unconscious mind shared by beings of the same species. LLMs are the first tangible manifestation of this. They are not thinking; they are accessing the averaged, weighted sum of human desire, fear, and logic. When an LLM predicts a geopolitical move or a consumer trend, it isn’t “guessing.” It is holding up a mirror to our collective species-level programming. It knows what we will do next because we are boringly consistent.

The Illusion of Executive Agency (Free Will vs. Math) This is the one that will upset the C-Suite. We love to believe in the “Great Man Theory”. That visionary leaders make unique choices that shape history. If an AI can predict your strategic pivot with 95% accuracy based on market vectors, did you make a choice? Or did you just execute the mathematically most probable next token in the sequence of “Capitalism”? Maybe we aren’t captains of the ship; we are variables in the equation. The most successful leaders of 2026 won’t be “visionaries”; they will be the ones who align their strategy with the highest-probability vector identified by the model.

The Practical Application: Scenario “Grading” But this newsletter has always been about practical application, about moving from metaphysics to Monday morning: If we accept that AI is a deterministic predictor, we stop asking it to write our plans and start asking it to grade our timelines.

  • Input: “Here is my strategic plan for Q3.”
  • Prompt: “Based on the last 50 years of SaaS market dynamics and current liquidity vectors, what is the probability of this sequence occurring?”
  • Output: “Low. The entropy of this plan ignores the high probability of [X] competitor reaction.”

Final Thought We are entering an era where “intuition” is demoted to “unverified data.” If the model says the future looks like X, and you feel it looks like Y, the burden of proof is now on you. The oracle isn’t magic; it’s just math. And the math says you’re predictable.
