Google Next 2026

I can’t remember the last time I was in Las Vegas for a conference, especially a Google one. It was probably 2021, right before I left on my startup journey. This year was billed as the biggest yet, with about 40,000 people at the Mandalay Bay Convention Center. It felt crowded, though I’m not convinced the number was that high. Still, it was unique.

I’m going to summarize how I see Google approaching the AI revolution, highlight some themes from the keynotes/breakouts/tech talks and exhibition, and share how this stacks up against the competition. In no particular order:

  • This was the first conference where I felt the battle for AI supremacy will be won in the user interface, not just in the foundation models. A solid foundational model is table stakes, but people will choose the tool that is easiest to use for everyday tasks. The company that owns the usability stack end to end will likely become the leader. (I doubt OpenAI or Anthropic are building email/docs/calendar/storage etc. anytime soon. They just can’t afford to.)
  • There are essentially two companies in this space: Google and Microsoft. Google appears to be unifying its AI tools and workspace productivity stack under a more cohesive usability approach. Called Gemini Enterprise Agent Platform (GEAP, Jeep? Dude…the name is terrible) This unified platform ensures that if you work within Google’s tech stack, you can integrate and build workflows with your own data with far less friction, without bouncing between multiple agent interfaces as before. The keynotes’ demos were slick, but we’ll see if they hold up in the real world of messy enterprise data and complex organizational structures.
  • Google announced a new open standard for agent-to-user interfaces, likely aiming to shape how UIs are built when workflows rely on multiple agents in a pipeline, such that you can input data and get answers without building a custom interface.
    https://developers.googleblog.com/introducing-a2ui-an-open-project-for-agent-driven-interfaces/
  • (In the other corner, Microsoft has integrated Copilot across much of its productivity stack. Does it actually give an experience and answers as solid as the other foundational models using the M365 plugins? Most would say, no. Microsoft may not mind, given its strong hold on the enterprise productivity market, but based on my daily use of Copilot versus what Google appears poised to deliver, there’s a clear winner.)
  • The conference exhibition hall was full of hands-on demos anyone could try. Security capture the flag, multi-agent orchestration, and more. These hands on demos used to be limited to the edges of the conference where the “l33t hakers” all congregated with the devrel folks in small huddled masses. No longer. There were plenty of small prizes, but the real takeaway is that Google seems to believe, and my observations (and experience) support, that the era of engineers writing code to solve every problem is shifting. LLMs are making many tasks easier.
  • Speaking of corners and edges. Security demos/products used to be tucked away in the back of the exhibition hall, but Google put security and observability front and center. It was a focal point across many of their exhibits, and plenty of other companies attending emphasized security too.
  • I saw many AI sec demos but couldn’t identify a common theme. Likely because I’m still unclear on what security looks like in an AI-first enterprise. I imagine individual agents are provisioned/permissioned like employees and run in sandboxed environments, with data access and egress permissions aligned to each workflow’s purpose. They’re provisioned on demand and auto-scaled. In some ways, it’s like Kubernetes for AI agents. Not necessarily the same technical structure, but conceptually similar. Still much to develop in this space.
  • They announced several updates to their security tools, but the unified platform still feels complex to me. It might be better suited to security focussed folks to get the most out of it.

New Specialized AI Security Agents: Introduced three new specialized agents within Google Security Operations: Threat Hunting Agent, Detection Engineering Agent, and Third-Party Context Agent to enhance security information.

AI Agent Governance and “Shadow AI” Protection: Launched new frameworks for managing agent risks, including Agent Gateway for central policy management, Agent Identity for tracking and enforcing least privilege, and Shadow AI Reporting to flag unmanaged AI tools.

Deepened Strategic Integration with Wiz: Embedded Wiz into the security stack via the AI Application Protection Platform (AI-APP), introducing the Wiz Red Agent for autonomous risk validation and AI-BOM (AI Bill of Materials) for securing AI-native development.

Writing this on the flight home, these are just first impressions. More details and integrations will likely emerge in the coming weeks. Overall, I was impressed by Google’s shift to an AI-first company and am excited to see where they go from here.

Things that make you go hmmm…

Given group statistics, it’s unsurprising that a large conference will include a wide spectrum of expertise in applying AI. There’s likely a high concentration of extremely smart, productive, and creative attendees. Still, a small experience at an executive dinner one evening made me reconsider the current AI adoption curve.

The AI tool I use most every day is an app called SuperWhisper (https://superwhisper.com/). Many readers have heard me mention it, and everyone in my daily life has endured my enthusiasm for how it boosts my productivity. At this dinner, I shared my enthusiasm and asked what speech-to-text, AI-based productivity tools people used. Not one person had really heard of them let alone used them in daily work. I was taken aback, since I’d assume they’d be incentivized to boost productivity. But adoption is logarithmic until saturation, and we early folks are still ahead of the curve. Consider this a hint: these tools can make an absolutely enormous difference — find one as soon as possible.

Still don’t know whos paying

Not that I expected otherwise, but my conversations with smaller startups around the conference hall make it clear: people still don’t know how to handle billing and entitlements. With flat-rate subscriptions likely fading and tiered token pricing becoming the norm, figuring out customer billing and entitlements (model access, token burn-down, prepaid, discounts, and other incentives across the customer base) is becoming urgent. The complexity is rising at an almost factorial rate.

Billing and Entitlements isn’t just a startup or customer issue. It also impacts large enterprise players. For big systems integrators like Accenture, PwC or Deloitte, a complex token-based future introduces real risks. Their clients fear open-ended AI operational costs, and SI’s delivering custom applications with unpredictable runtime spend creates major liability.

A billing and entitlements layer, embedded as a foundational pillar in the reference architecture’s coordination layer, is the most viable path. It enables dynamic model routing and internal chargebacks across business units. If only there was a solution… https://schematichq.com/

Tough Advice for Enterprise

I’m tucking this section at the end for my most devoted readers. If you’re in enterprise (non-techish) and enthusiastic about AI, whether you’re already ahead of the curve or just taking your first step, this part is for you.

AI has enormous potential to transform your workforce, accelerate operations, and improve employees’ work-life balance. That said, today’s flat, per-seat subscription pricing may be masking significant challenges ahead.

Microsoft announced that its Copilot AI tool for VS Code and other IDEs is shifting to token-only pricing. https://www.wheresyoured.at/exclusive-microsoft-moving-all-github-copilot-subscribers-to-token-based-billing-in-june/ While I haven’t seen much uproar (yet), it’s a widely used, global tool and could be a fat canary in the coal mine for how companies will charge for AI tools going forward. Foundational labs like Anthropic, Google, OpenAI and (by extension) Microsoft will all shift to per‑token pricing for AI tools, which will likely push many wrapper tools to do the same. This trend also creates a tailwind for self‑hosting and open‑source models (but that’s a more technical topic for another time.)

If your enterprise has already deployed AI to a large share of employees, you need to assess the total cost — currently affordable but likely to become very substantial. If there’s a measurable ROI for your business from broad AI use, the next step is identifying who can deliver the most efficient per-token inference for your company.

You have two paths:

- Identify roles with high usage and clear ROI on specific projects. Likely focus AI on engineering or data-heavy operations, and choose models and budgets carefully. Everyone else goes back to pre-AI workflow methods.

- If you want AI for the entire workforce, determine who can deliver the most cost-effective integrated environment.

If you choose the second option,we’re right back to the start of this week’s newsletter. You have two paths: Microsoft or Google.

Infra (data centers/chips), potential efficiency gains (in-house model development and DeepMind talent), and product integration currently favor Google. Microsoft has largely bet on acquiring OpenAI’s IP and serving it on external GPUs. Looking ahead, Google’s deeper integration and engineering may give it the edge for an AI-enabled workforce. Microsoft will try to keep pace, but structurally they’ve made their choices, and pivoting to mirror Google’s playbook at this point means starting from pretty far back. In a per-token future, Google wins.
