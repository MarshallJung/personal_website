A Tough Choice, A Bitter Pill

If you have successfully integrated​ even one agent into your core workflows, you are already operating in the top tier of AI practitioners​ (like the top 1%). While consumer adoption dominates headlines, the far more significant shift is occurring inside the enterprise. The widening divide between basic prompt-and-response users and those constructing compounding, high-leverage workflows.

Enterprise adoption consistently mirrors a power-law curve. 5–10% of an organization naturally emerges as high-leverage builders driving the vast majority of token consumption and business impact, while the broader workforce interacts superficially with general tooling.

https://x.com/arpit_bhayani/status/2085207537843077396?s=20
https://x.com/MBeierschoder/status/2083844697143808210?s=20

This gap exists because building agentic leverage is an exacting engineering craft, not a general office skill. It requires:

  • Rigorous Context Management: Curating compact, high-signal prompt windows and state persistence.
  • Modular Skill Files & Tooling: Equipping models with deterministic API boundaries and clear execution constraints.
  • Deterministic vs. Stochastic Routing: Systematically deciding where rule-based logic must govern and where probabilistic reasoning should take over.
  • Differential Reviews: Auditing model outputs against ground-truth systems rather than relying on qualitative spot-checks.

Because this craft cannot scale through generic prompt webinars, treating AI as a decentralized personal productivity tool quickly hits a plateau. Capturing asymmetric value requires deliberate organizational disruption.

To build enterprise capability, organizations must assemble their curious, first-principled generalists (they’ll be currently working across supply chain, R&D, finance, operations etc.) into a unified leadership and engineering structure.

This transition inevitably creates friction across long-standing operating models:

  • Talent Reallocation: Pulling top-tier problem solvers out of traditional silos directly challenges legacy team compositions and departmental roadmaps.
  • Redefining Ownership: Shifting from isolated functional databases to a unified, governed ontology forces teams to surrender territorial data boundaries.
  • Workflow Deconstruction: Long-standing, manually intensive processes must be systematically pulled apart and rebuilt into human-in-the-loop agentic architectures.

Disrupting familiar structures and reallocating resources and budget across deeply entrenched departmental boundaries is uncomfortable, and it cannot happen from the bottom up. It requires direct, committed executive sponsorship to provide air cover and redefine performance metrics around unified execution.

The organizations willing to navigate this disruption trade calcified legacy routines for an agile, compounding operating engine. One that structurally lowers operating costs, accelerates decision cycles, and turns localized technical craft into an enduring enterprise moat.

Make no mistake, these changes are hard; there’s no way to sugarcoat it. Unless the changes are owned and led by the very top senior leaders, AI will make little difference to your bottom line.

I take umbrage

https://www.anthropic.com/news/claude-text-watermark

On Friday, Anthropic announced it will watermark all generated text using the methods linked above. While there’s debate about its long-term effectiveness, I’m more concerned about the approach itself.

They say watermarking will involve swapping out language choices a user might submit while working with their models. So if you ask it to proofread or edit an email or document, it may change words to satisfy the watermarking algorithm. As someone who values traditional writing, precise word choice matters. I think lawyers would agree (see: O’Connor v. Oakhurst Dairy (2017), U.S. Court of Appeals for the 1st Circuit). Algorithmic substitutions risk altering an author’s intended meaning.

I’m also worried about ownership. Will Anthropic use watermarking to claim that generated text is authored by (and thus owned by) Anthropic? In a business context, if you use it to write product docs or code that helps generate profit, does Anthropic claim any ownership of that profit by its “contribution”? These raise serious legal questions that could make businesses hesitate to become customers.

But what really bothers me is Anthropic’s apparent hypocrisy and lack of patriotism. The U.S. government asked to use their models for defensive purposes, and Anthropic refused. Yet this watermarking tech exists because of EU law. So when the EU says jump, Anthropic asks “how high?” That feels deeply distasteful from a company born in the United States and benefiting from our free markets and technology-first culture.

Skills Everywhere

Lately, I’ve found it really helpful to visualize code — and projects in general. I’m a very visual learner, so asking my AI tools to create visual representations of how a process or code repository works helps me understand the system and troubleshoot issues more effectively.

https://x.com/mattpocockuk/status/2088290952704151671?s=20

AI engineer and blogger Matt Pocock released a 10-minute video summarizing his AI coding skills for engineering. While some are too technical for project managers or other non-coding knowledge workers, many are highly transferable and useful. My takeaway is skills should be specific in language, yet generic enough in their technology references to stay transferable across different models, harnesses, and over time.

A True Co-worker?

https://learn.chatgpt.com/docs/customization/computer-history

Companies like Mercor hire industry specialists to perform non-AI assisted knowledge work and package the “recording” of those skills for large AI labs. The premise is that experts in esoteric tasks (such as SAP or CAD or making good slide decks?) can train LLMs to perform these tasks, enabling the models to learn closely related processes in the same or similar tools.

OpenAI now allows its harnes to capture details about your workflows to help complete tasks better and faster. Ideally, this is a fully granular approach (similar to what training providers offer) but tailored to each individual. The models aren’t fine-tuned on your specific tasks or method but use distilled context to improve performance.

I haven’t tried this yet, but there are two things to watch out for. I don’t know how this affects consumption (I assume it doesn’t reduce it, because ofc it won’t). And I’m unsure about the effectiveness of the methodologies. How they’re verified and how they help. REmains to be seen.

Valuable?

https://cdn.openai.com/pdf/how-organizations-use-chatgpt.pdf

This paper suggests AI use may boost firm performance. Early evidence indicates that firms adopting AI early and heavily were already performing well and are likely to keep outpacing competitors. OpenAI’s data shows some firms use AI more broadly and deeply, and these firms tend to have the most productive employees. But there may be reverse causality: highly productive firms may have work that’s inherently well suited to AI. Heavy AI use often reflects strong process clarity and that clarity was compounding even before LLMs arrived.
