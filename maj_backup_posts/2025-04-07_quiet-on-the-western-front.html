Quiet on the Western Front?

It’s likely true across various fields that those at the forefront find some place where cutting edge insights about the latest developments. In the AI space, significant news, new models, and interesting papers are typically announced on X, a.k.a Twitter. Staying updated in the AI space can be intentional through curated follow lists and relevant hashtags/keywords. However, it often relies on instinct, making it difficult to guarantee that I have the latest information. In the past few weeks I’ve noticed that the pace of announcements in the AI startup world seems to have slowed down a bit. I can’t say for sure if that’s the case, but that’s my observation. Please take it with a grain of salt.

That isn’t to say that nothing interesting has been happening. There have been some interesting developments: Google has released Gemini 2.5 (a few weeks ago), and today, Meta launched new Lama models.

I have to admit that my initial predictions were wrong, though I’ve admitted this before. It seems that Google’s deliberate pre-training has elevated their 2.5 model to a much higher level of capability for a foundational model. This sets an intriguing benchmark for AI model performance versus cost. Notably, the cost for a 4th level model, which we can also refer to as GPT-4 class models, has dropped by 99.7%, while even the most advanced models are still 82% cheaper in the last two years. I’m curious to see where this trend will lead in the future, and I’m no longer betting against it.

Here are a few observations before we dive into today’s newsletter topic. First, it’s curious that Microsoft seems to be absent from these discussions. While they are quietly enhancing Copilot within their tools and cloud offerings, they appear less interested in pushing the boundaries of model training or marketing. Sure they are still frienemies with OpenAI, but they seem to be putting no effort into research or innovation.

Additionally, despite Google’s impressive engineering and algorithmic capabilities, they struggle with marketing and getting the word out about their strengths. It’s surprising, and I believe there are many who could help solve this issue. I’m willing to assist, but there are probably even better candidates out there!

Go Touch Grass

The most interesting thing to me this last week was a paper titled ai-2027, released by Scott Alexander and former alignment researchers from OpenAI.

https://ai-2027.com/ Podcast: https://youtu.be/htOvH12T7mU?si=6vuZ8ymZ_6sa_3fb

I say it was the most interesting, but really it was the most infuriating; it truly embodies the definition of Silicon Valley tech bro navel gazing. https://x.com/marshalljung/status/1908180963466547589

The paper predicts a near-future “Intelligence Explosion,” potentially kicking off around 2027. The core idea? AI gets really good at researching…AI. This recursive loop, apparently, spins up everything from superhuman coders to god-like AI researchers faster than you can say “compute bottleneck.”

It’s a compelling narrative. We start with glorified chatbots (their words, roughly!) and end up with superintelligence optimizing every widget in sight, directing vast robot economies, and tiling the planet with solar panels and factories. Sounds efficient!

Here’s where I raise a skeptical eyebrow. While the scenario waxes poetic about algorithms begetting better algorithms, it seems to conveniently sidestep the messy, inconvenient realities needed to actually make things happen in the physical world. They mention compute being a bottleneck (fair enough) and eventually envision robots needing raw materials and energy. But the initial rocket fuel for this explosion seems to be purely digital optimisation, as if hyper-intelligent software can somehow magically conjure the power grids, chip fabs, mines, and global logistics required to support its own exponential growth before it takes over the physical means of production.

It feels a bit like planning a transcontinental railroad by focusing solely on perfecting the train schedule, while giving only a passing nod to the trivial matters of laying track, digging tunnels, or sourcing steel.

They acknowledge it’s speculative (“much of this is guesswork,” they concede), and aim for a “conservative” take (which feels… ironic, given the outcome). And yes, they touch on compute limits and the eventual need for robot armies to manage resources. But the core engine — AI improving AI in a research context — seems surprisingly divorced from the grubby details of engineering, resource scarcity, and the sheer friction of the real world, especially in the crucial early stages.

My take? It’s a fascinating thought experiment about the potential speed of purely algorithmic progress. But projecting that onto the complex, resource-constrained physical world without fully grappling with the practical hurdles feels… well, let’s just call it optimistic. Maybe the superintelligent AI’s first breakthrough needs to be figuring out how to actually build its own house before it redesigns the universe.

Not that I want to end on a sad note…but

Many of my readers know I’m a car enthusiast. I enjoy race cars, car engineering, and high-performance vehicles. I often spend my spare time watching YouTube videos about building and designing race cars, which I find fascinating. Recently, I’ve noticed my feed has been filled with AI-generated cars that are often unrealistic and paired with amusingly inaccurate AI voiceovers. For anyone with even a basic understanding of vehicles, this can be quite off-putting. Many YouTube channels are primarily AI-generated, releasing hundreds or even thousands of videos each week. There are countless channels producing low-quality content. It’s not limited to cars; some are using voice clones of well-known inspirational figures from the internet. See this link: https://www.youtube.com/@RiseWithPurpose-s2w This feels like self-help slop, which is gross. It seems like they just produce content for algorithmic attention and profit farming. It’s frustrating to see poor AI-generated cars, but it’s even more disappointing when they try and generate human wisdom.

As I’ve mentioned in this newsletter many times before, authenticity will be the most valuable commodity in the world of AI. Being truly human, showing you care and can help is the most valuable commodity. Take time away from electronics and dare I say it, go touch some grass. I encourage everyone to step back and reflect on what makes us unique and fulfilled. Hug your friends, tell your family you love them, and maintain a sense of realism as we navigate this new AI-driven world.
