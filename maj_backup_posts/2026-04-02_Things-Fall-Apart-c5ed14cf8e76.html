Things Fall Apart

April 02, 2026

Note: I’m releasing my Monday morning newsletter early this week to put a stake in the ground on the open source security issues currently plaguing the AI development environment. It’s mostly opinion, but any facts are easily verifiable via X.com or other up-to-the-minute tech reporters.

Things Fall Apart

About two weeks ago, a targeted exploit hit Trivy — the open-source vulnerability scanner that half the planet runs in its CI/CD pipelines — then rippled straight into LiteLLM, the lightweight Python library and proxy server that gives you a single OpenAI-compatible interface to over 100 LLM providers. I wrote about the opening act this last Monday. Since then the cascade has accelerated: the same threat actor (TeamPCP) used stolen CI credentials to poison Checkmarx’s KICS GitHub Actions, spin up CanisterWorm (a self-propagating npm payload that infected 47+ packages in under 60 seconds using an ICP blockchain canister as an untakable dead-drop C2), and slip two malicious versions of LiteLLM (1.82.7 and 1.82.8) onto PyPI.

The outcomes are still unfolding, but they’re already ugly. Developers who pulled the bad versions handed over the literal keys to their AI stacks — think production agents that can now be impersonated, fine-tuned models exfiltrated, or entire RAG pipelines poisoned at the source. Downstream, any project treating LiteLLM as a transitive dependency woke up to a silent backdoor. And because the same credential harvest fed CanisterWorm, dozens of unrelated npm packages started auto-publishing their own malicious updates, turning the supply chain into a game of cryptographic whack-a-mole.

Whether this surge stems from AI-generated pull requests, a toxic blend of human oversight and LLM-slop code review, maintainer fatigue, or just the sheer velocity of “ship it before the next token,” the result is the same. Dependency hell has graduated from annoying to near-cataclysmic. We used to live in a world where open-source supply chains were an inconvenient truth. But because code was slow and lots of eyeballs grokked PRs, issues were rare. Now os-libs are the easy route for bad actors. Human nature being what it is, greedy, lazy, and occasionally malicious, we don’t get to ignore that anymore. I still don’t know exactly why the wave crested right now, but it was as inevitable as a Douglas Adams character realizing the answer to life, the universe, and everything is 42 and the question was never asked properly.

This feels tragic, and I hope I’m overreacting. I live for the moment a model chains 17 tool calls in a row without hallucinating its own API keys. But right now AI development velocity is being held hostage by the very poisoned registries that once felt like a quirky cost of doing business. The high of building useful apps is still unmatched; the low of wondering whether your next uv install just mailed your Anthropic keys to models.litellm[.]cloud is a special kind of soul-crushing.

At this point the major frontier labs (OpenAI, Anthropic, xAI, Google DeepMind, Meta, Microsoft) need to do something that actually matches the scale of the problem they ignited. Jointly bankroll a serious, no-nonsense security research collective whose sole job is to harden the open-source AI ecosystem before it collapses under its own weight. Not another whitepaper. Not another “best practices” blog post. Actual engineering muscle: formal verification pipelines that treat AI-generated code as suspect until proven otherwise (at least SMT solvers that can reason about intent); advanced static analysis tools that understand LLM intent, i.e., they don’t just pattern-match for “os.system,” they model whether a prompt-to-payload path could ever exfiltrate secrets; autonomous fuzzing at planetary scale across the full attack surface (prompt chains → tool calls → dependency graphs → runtime memory); and next-gen supply chain integrity layers purpose-built for AI-native workflows with cryptographic provenance for every generated artifact, SBOMs that track model lineage, reproducible builds that refuse to run unless every transitive dep is signed and pinned.

Yes, this will be expensive. The labs have already over-allocated capex to inference hardware because that’s the glamorous, GPU-shaped part of the story. But here’s the Thomas Sowell bit: incentives matter. If no one trusts the dependencies, no one ships production apps, and the ROI on those clusters evaporates faster than a jailbroken llama.cpp binary on a public cloud. The ecosystem they lit on fire deserves better than “move fast and hope the dependencies don’t get backdoored.” Secure the golden goose before the next XZ-utils-level incident turns the entire AI party into a smoldering crater.

A few additional issues the headlines have glossed over: First, the transitive-dependency blast radius is now absurd. LiteLLM is more than just a library. It’s the universal translator sitting in the middle of thousands of agent frameworks. One compromised proxy and you’ve pwned the entire multi-LLM orchestration layer. Second, AI itself is accelerating both sides. Models are now writing kernel exploits in hours and flooding bug bounties with slop, which means the review burden on human maintainers is doubling while their attention is halving. Third, the economic tragedy of the commons is in full bloom. Individual maintainers can’t afford enterprise-grade secret hygiene or formal verification, so the whole open-source commons becomes the attack surface. And finally, the social cost. eroding trust goes beyond just technical debt, it causes talent flight. Why grind on a PyPI package when you could join a well-funded closed-source shop with actual security theater? (Anthropic’s oops notwithstanding, but more on that later) Startups will pay higher insurance, investors will demand punitive due diligence, and the collaborative magic that made AI explode could quietly calcify into “only trust what the big labs sign.”

We don’t need more sermons about moving slower. We need the people who lit the fuse to fund the fire extinguishers. Do it before the whole village burns down and we’re left explaining to the next generation why the golden age of open AI lasted roughly as long as a decent prompt. Let’s build the security layer the frontier deserves. The alternative is just too predictably, hilariously, tragically doomed.

Own Yourself

Just as the supply-chain pyre from Trivy, LiteLLM, and CanisterWorm was still smoldering, Anthropic managed the neat trick of accidentally shipping its own Claude Code source. 512,000 lines of pristine TypeScript across 1,900 files — straight into the public npm registry. Version 2.1.88 of @anthropic-ai/claude-code arrived on March 31 with a 59.8 MB cli.js.map file that no one had bothered to exclude. One forgotten .npmignore entry, one Bun.js default that loves generating production source maps, and suddenly the entire agentic coding harness was downloadable. Anthropic’s legal team fired off DMCA notices faster than a prompt can hallucinate, but too late! The code was already mirrored, forked 80,000 times, and clean-room ported to Python and Rust. Someone even spun up “openclaude” to make the whole thing model-agnostic. Supply-chain attacks meet self-own: same week, same registries, different flavor of “whoops.”

The irony is thicker than a Douglas Adams footnote. Anthropic has proudly declared that most of its code these days is written by Claude itself. AI-assisted velocity at its finest. Yet here we are, watching an unmanaged build pipeline (human error, they called it) do what no bad actor could…hand the crown jewels to the world. The very tool designed to churn out production-grade code couldn’t stop itself from leaking its own source files because a regex for “.map” never made it into the release checklist. Claude Code could, in theory, have released its own source without human intervention; but more likely instead, the humans forgot to tell the build system “don’t do that.” Thomas Sowell would nod knowingly: incentives matter, and the incentive to ship fast apparently outranked the incentive to treat your own agent like the untrusted intern it secretly is.

What the leak actually revealed is pure catnip. Anti-distillation poison pills, fake tools and decoy APIs sprinkled throughout the codebase to mislead any competitor foolish enough to train on Claude’s outputs. Undercover Mode, a stealth layer that scrubs every trace of “Claude” or “Anthropic” from git commits and PRs so the AI-generated code looks reassuringly, boringly human. A frustration detector. Regexes scanning prompts for profanity and rage-typing so the system can quietly log when a user is about to yeet their laptop out the window. And then the roadmap easter eggs. Buddy, a Tamagotchi-style digital pet that perches in your terminal and reacts to your coding mood. Kairos, the background daemon (Greek for the exact, divinely appointed moment, in this case ironically NOT determined by Anthropic management (“Amodei” for the win right?)) that keeps a daily journal and slips into “dream mode” to consolidate memories while you sleep. Unreleased model codenames (Opus 4.7, Sonnet 4.8, the mysterious Capybara) plus modes labeled “ultra plan,” “coordinator,” and “daemon.” All the future, neatly commented and suddenly public.

This is the same week we were already uneasy about poisoned npm/PyPI packages. Now the very frontier lab that preaches safety-first has demonstrated, in real time, how even the most sophisticated AI-assisted process can still be felled by the oldest unmanaged one: “just ship it.” The economic clarity is brutal. Every fork, every port, every competitor quietly studying the orchestration loop erodes the moat faster than any regulation ever could. The social cost is subtler. The open-source community that once revered Anthropic as the responsible adult in the room now gets to treat its crown jewel like just another npm package. And the business impact? Well, when your $multi-billion-a-year coding agent becomes the fastest-growing GitHub repo in history for all the wrong reasons, the golden goose starts looking a little plucked.

Like I already said, I live for the moment an agent nails 17 tool calls in a row. I just don’t think Anthropic engineers expected one of them would be “expose our entire source map to the internet.” Here’s hoping the next Kairos daemon arrives with better build hygiene than the last one. Otherwise we’re all just waiting for the universe to hand us the answer 42 and realize, too late, that the question was “how do we not accidentally publish the thing we spent years keeping secret?”
