My dear readers will need to forgive me this week. I will include a few useful tools and interesting papers that I’ve encountered, but there is some introspection that needs to be done now that we are in 2025. Of course looking inward need not be only at the start of a new year. Ideally we are always working on our inner selves, but external milestones like staying up past midnight once a year (yes, I’m old) is as good a time as any. And saying that…

At the start of a new year, it is beneficial to contemplate the nature of time. We often perceive time as moving — and its pace seems to accelerate with each passing year. It is akin to a river that flows unidirectionally. However, time itself does not move; rather, we traverse through it.

This concept is analogous to other dimensions. When we are driving, the scenery appears to rush by, but we recognize that it is stationary; we are the ones in motion. Therefore, it is logical to apply this same understanding to time.

Just as objects appear to move faster when we are driving at a high speed, time feels accelerated when we process information rapidly. When we rely on existing knowledge and expectations, we overlook the intricacies of how we construct our comprehension of the world. This allows us to skim over sensory data and arrive at conclusions more quickly. We experience this as time speeding up. However, we can decelerate this perception by focusing on the details and building our understanding from the ground up. Perhaps in 2025, we can endeavor to slow down a little. Life, and all its remarkable details, are worthwhile…

Do I use AI to write this newsletter?

I’m often asked if I use AI to write this newsletter. The answer is no, at least not for the actual words that readers see on a weekly basis. (The one exception is the interesting experiment where I asked AI to grade my 2024 predictions. I’ll always disclose any AI generated content.) There are at least two reasons why I physically type these paragraphs every week. The first is what I referred to in my last newsletter. Discipline. There are lots of benefits to writing everything myself. Focus, accountability, pride in my work, and the well proven effect that doing your own writing about a particular subject is unparalleled in helping understand the nuances of that subject. I get smarter by writing. And by writing consistently, I retain more of what I learned. Using AI would get me the words, but I would not really understand the subject. The second reason is that AI just can’t sound like me. I’ve been waiting for the real-time fine-tuned/CoT types of models that can replicate the style of my writing, but it just can’t. Google has said that they are working towards a NotebookLM feature that will use a writer’s works to replicate their patterns, but as of now there isn’t really something that does this. There is a bunch of internet slop out there, and it’s hilarious (or horrifying). If I really want the AI to explain ARC-AGI benchmarks to me in the style of Bukowski, it probably can do that. But somehow it’s not authentic. Not genuine. The currency of the future is being real. How hard it will be for me to maintain resolve and relevance in the face of AI…I’m not sure yet.

And on the subject of AI slop

I can see the future of AI, especially the image and video models like SORA and Veo2 being used to generate mesmerizing, short form content (warning: creepy https://x.com/DrClownPhD/status/1855940765165273578) that add absolutely nothing to the advancement of humanity. And let’s not even get into the life-like AI that is indistinguishable from real people.

But let me end with something positive. I find that YouTube is one of the most valuable learning resources out there. From AI tutorials, to people building the most amazing things in home garages, it’s the last bastion of real and raw creativity. I recently installed this chrome extension https://chromewebstore.google.com/detail/longform-youtube-filter/aldjiffocdadfjmocpmifobjobkfalke?hl=en which allows me to filter out all the short form videos. Finding the best of the internet is still possible and I do find that YouTube honors my algorithm. They won’t inject brain rot, especially when I filter out the short form slop. Slow progress

So back in july zoo.dev released a demo of text-to-CAD. I covered it in the newsletter. It was a basic plate with some radiused edges and an inset pocket. January 1st, they posted again https://x.com/j_d_noone/status/1874571809716384069. This time the plate has received some through holes and been extruded. For 6 months of work, this isn’t particularly inspiring. Now, I’m not competing with them, so it’s hard for me to throw stones, but in comparison with, say text-to-video, this isn’t much progress. I have proposed a benchmark. The McMaster Carr bolt benchmark. When you can describe the following in text and receive a 3D model consistent with reality I’ll be impressed.

Ask and it shall be given

https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.01992

This paper really tickles me as it reinforces my idea of language being the true tool of AI. Turns out, it’s all in how you ask the question. Prompting is super-powerful. A single finite transformer can compute anything, just by changing its prompts. So my AI language “Socrato”(™) is one step closer to being reality.

Necessity is the Mother of Invention

When everyone wants you to leetcode interview, what is the answer? https://github.com/ibttf/interview-coder It’s basically invisible and gives help in realtime to coding interviews. I like that tools such as this exist. I’m very dependent on AI to help me with coding, and at any job that I might have I would expect to be able to use any appropriate AI tools to be more effective. I think it’s more important that a company understands how a potential employee uses AI rather than their capabilities without.

Tool Time!

- Browser Use
https://github.com/browser-use/browser-use

It’s good, but important to know how it works and the tradeoffs. It takes a screenshot of the page and sends it with the tree index to LLM to decide the next step for the agent. Quite expensive if you are using an API and a bit slow but works well if you’re willing to make that trade off.

- Agent Coordination
https://github.com/Thytu/Agentarium

Agents are a thing. Are they a good thing? Maybe, but for sure it’s a pain to direct traffic and tasks amongst multiple agents. This repo is an effort to solve that coordination problem.

Or if you prefer a major’s take:
https://github.com/awslabs/multi-agent-orchestrator

AWS has released basically the same thing. I have not used the AWS version yet, but I think if anyone was interested in this, it might be down to understanding where the long term support might come from. I hate to say this might be AWS, but hey we gotta run production too.
